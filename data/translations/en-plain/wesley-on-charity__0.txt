0.1	“If I speak in the languages of people and of angels, but do not have charity, I have become like a booming piece of brass or a jingling cymbal.
0.2	And if I have the gift of prophecy, and understand all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all faith, so that I can move mountains, but do not have charity, I am nothing.
0.3	And if I give away everything I own to feed the poor, and give my body to be burned, but do not have charity, it does me no good at all.”
0.4	1 Corinthians 13:1–3.
0.5	We know that “all Scripture is given by God’s inspiration.” So it is true and right about everything.
0.6	But we also know that some parts of Scripture speak more directly to everyone’s conscience.
0.7	The passage in front of us belongs to this group. Hardly anyone objects to it.
0.8	In fact, most people are very quick to appeal to it.
0.9	It is very common to find people who deny the authority of the Holy Scriptures, but still say, “This is my religion: what is described in the thirteenth chapter of Corinthians.”
0.10	Even a Jewish man, Dr. Nunes, a Spanish doctor who had settled at Savannah in Georgia, used to say very earnestly, “Paul of Tarsus was one of the finest writers I have ever read.
0.11	I wish the thirteenth chapter of his first letter to the Corinthians were written in letters of gold.
0.12	And I wish every Jew would carry it with him wherever he went.”
0.13	He believed that this one chapter contained the whole of true religion. And he was certainly right about that.
0.14	It contains “whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely. If there is any virtue, if there is anything worth praising,” it is all contained in this.
0.15	To see this as clearly as possible, we can consider the following:
0.16	I. What the charity spoken of here is.
0.17	II. What things people usually put in its place.
0.18	Then we can,
0.19	III. Show that none of these things, not even all of them together, can make up for not having it.
0.20	I. 1. First, we need to consider what this charity is.
0.21	What is it like, and what are its qualities?
0.22	The word Paul uses is agape. It means exactly the same as the plain English word love.
0.23	That is why all the old translations of the Bible used the word love.
0.24	This is how it read in William Tyndale’s Bible. I believe that was the first English translation of the whole Bible.
0.25	It was the same in the Bible published by the authority of King Henry VIII.
0.26	It was also the same in all the editions of the Bible published in England, one after another, during the reigns of King Edward VI, Queen Elizabeth, and King James I. In fact, you can find it in the Bibles printed while King Charles I reigned. I believe this was so right up to the end of his reign.
0.27	The first Bibles I have seen where the word was changed were printed in 1649 by Roger Daniel and John Field, the printers for Parliament. So it seems likely that the change was made while the Long Parliament was meeting. That is probably when the Latin word charity replaced the English word love.
0.28	It was a sad day when this change was made. Its bad effects are still with us today. You can see them among poor people and people who cannot read: thousands of ordinary men and women understand the word charity no better than they understand the original Greek. But the same terrible mistake has also spread among educated and learned people.
0.29	Thousands of them are misled by it. They imagine that the charity this chapter talks about is mainly, or even only, about outward actions. They think it means little more than giving money to the poor!
0.30	I have heard many sermons preached on this chapter, especially before the University of Oxford.
0.31	And I only ever heard one in which its meaning was not completely misrepresented.
0.32	But if the old and proper word love had been kept, no one could have misrepresented it.
0.33	2. But what kind of love is the apostle talking about all through this chapter?
0.34	Many people who are very learned and godly believe it is love for God.
0.35	But I have read the whole chapter countless times and looked at it from every angle. And I am fully convinced that Paul is speaking here directly about love for our neighbour.
0.36	I believe anyone who carefully considers the whole flow of what he says will be fully convinced of this.
0.37	But we must admit that it is a kind of love for our neighbour that can only come from love for God.
0.38	And where does this love for God come from? It comes only from the faith that is God’s own work. Whoever has this faith has direct evidence that God was in Christ, bringing the world back into friendship with himself.
0.39	Suppose this is applied personally to his heart, so that he can say with humble boldness: The life I now live, I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me. Then, and not until then, the love of God is poured out in his heart.
0.40	And this love sweetly compels him to love every human being with the love spoken of here. This is not a love of admiration or of approval. That kind of love has no place toward those who are enemies of God and of their own souls, even if they are not his personal enemies. No, it is a love of goodwill—a tender goodwill toward every soul that God has made.
0.41	3. But someone may ask: “Suppose there is no true love for our neighbour except the love that comes from loving God. And suppose love for God comes from no other source than faith in the Son of God. Doesn’t that mean the whole heathen world has no possible way to be saved?
0.42	After all, they are cut off from faith. For faith comes by hearing, and how can they hear without a preacher?”
0.43	I answer that Paul’s words, spoken about a different matter, apply here too: “What the law says, it says to those who are under the law.”
0.44	In the same way, the sentence “Whoever does not believe will be damned” is spoken about those who have had the Gospel preached to them. It does not concern anyone else. And we are not asked to decide anything about their final state.
0.45	We can leave it to God himself to decide how he, the Judge of all, will deal with them.
0.46	But this much we know. He is not only the God of Christians; he is also the God of the heathen. He is “rich in mercy to all who call on him,” according to the light they have. And “in every nation, whoever fears God and does what is right is accepted by him.”
0.47	4. But let’s get back to the subject.
0.48	This is the nature of the love the apostle is talking about here.
0.49	But what are its qualities? What fruits always go together with it?
0.50	The apostle lists many of them. But these are the main ones.
0.51	First: “Love is not puffed up.”
0.52	However much love we have, that is how much humility we have.
0.53	Nothing humbles the soul as deeply as love does. It drives out all “high opinions of ourselves that breed pride.” It drives out all arrogance and conceit. It makes us small and poor and lowly and worthless in our own eyes.
0.54	It humbles us before God and before people. It makes us willing to be the least of all and the servant of all. And it teaches us to say, “A speck of dust in a sunbeam is small, but in the presence of God I am infinitely smaller.”
0.55	5. Second: “Love is not provoked.” Our present English translation says, “is not easily provoked.”
0.56	But where did the word easily come from?
0.57	There is not a trace of it in the text. The apostle’s words are simply these: ou paraxynetai.
0.58	Isn’t it likely that the translators added it to excuse Paul? Perhaps they were afraid that what he did would seem to contradict what he taught.
0.59	For we read in Acts 15:36 and the verses after it: “Some days later Paul said to Barnabas, Let’s go back and visit our brothers in every city where we have preached the word of the Lord, and see how they are doing.
0.60	Barnabas decided to take John, who was also called Mark, with them.
0.61	But Paul did not think it was a good idea to take someone who had left the work.
0.62	The quarrel between them was so sharp that they parted from each other. Barnabas took Mark and sailed to Cyprus. Paul chose Silas and left, and the brothers entrusted him to the grace of God.
0.63	And he traveled through Syria and Cilicia, strengthening the churches.”
0.64	6. When you read these words, wouldn’t you think they were both equally sharp?
0.65	Wouldn’t you think Paul was just as heated as Barnabas, and just as lacking in love?
0.66	But the text says no such thing. This becomes clear if we first look at what led to it.
0.67	When Paul suggested that they should “go back and visit the brothers in every city where they had preached the word,” they were in agreement so far.
0.68	“Barnabas decided to take John with them,” because John was his sister’s son. He did this without asking for Paul’s advice or getting it.
0.69	“But Paul did not think it was right to take him along, since he had left them in Pamphylia”—whether out of laziness or cowardice—“and had not gone on with them to the work.”
0.70	And Paul was surely right. Reason was on his side.
0.71	The next words are egento oun paroxysmos. Literally they mean, “and there was a fit of anger.”
0.72	It does not say that this anger was in Paul. Probably it was in Barnabas alone. He made up for his lack of reason with passion, “so that they separated from each other.”
0.73	Barnabas was determined to have his own way. So he did what his nephew had done before: he “left the work,” “took Mark with him, and sailed to Cyprus.”
0.74	But Paul carried on with his work, “entrusted by the brothers to the grace of God.” Barnabas does not seem to have waited for that.
0.75	“And he traveled through Syria and Cilicia, strengthening the churches.”
0.76	From the whole story, it does not seem that Paul did anything wrong. He did not have any feeling, or say any word, that went against the law of love.
0.77	So since he did nothing wrong, he does not need any excuse.
0.78	7. Certainly, someone who is full of love is “gentle toward everyone.”
0.79	He “gently teaches those who oppose him.” They oppose what he loves most—the truth of God, or the holiness without which no one will see the Lord. For who knows? “Perhaps God will bring them to know the truth.”
0.80	No matter how much he is provoked, he does “not pay back evil for evil, or insult for insult.”
0.81	Yes, he “blesses those who curse him, and does good to those who treat him cruelly and persecute him.”
0.82	He “is not overcome by evil, but” always “overcomes evil with good.”
0.83	8. Third: “Love suffers long.”
0.84	It puts up with more than a few insults, criticisms, and wrongs. It puts up with everything that God allows people or devils to do to it.
0.85	It arms the soul with a patience that cannot be broken. This is not the hard patience of the Stoics. It is a patience that gives way like the air. The air does not resist a blow, so it is not harmed by it.
0.86	The person who loves all people remembers the One who suffered for us, “leaving us an example, so that we might walk in his steps.”
0.87	So, “if his enemy is hungry, he feeds him; if he is thirsty, he gives him something to drink.” By doing this, he “heaps burning coals” of melting love on his head.
0.88	“Many waters cannot put out this love, and the floods” of ingratitude “cannot drown it.”
0.89	II. 1. Second, we need to ask what things people commonly think can take the place of love.
0.90	The first of these is eloquence. This is the ability to speak well, especially about religious subjects.
0.91	People usually tend to think well of someone who speaks well.
0.92	If he speaks about God and the things of God properly and fluently, who could doubt that God is pleased with him?
0.93	And it is very natural for him to think well of himself too, and to have as high an opinion of himself as others have.
0.94	2. But thoughtful people are not satisfied with this. A flood of words is not enough for them. They value thinking more than talking. They believe that someone who knows a lot is far better than someone who talks a lot.
0.95	And knowledge certainly is an excellent gift from God. This is especially true of knowing the Holy Scriptures, which contain all the depths of divine knowledge and wisdom.
0.96	So people generally think that a person with a lot of knowledge, especially knowledge of Scripture, must not only have God’s favour but must have a large share of it.
0.97	3. But people who think even more deeply often say, “I don’t put any weight on any knowledge except knowing God by faith.
0.98	Faith is the only knowledge that is of great value in God’s sight.
0.99	‘We are saved by faith’—by faith alone. This is the one thing we need.
0.100	Whoever believes, and only that person, will be saved forever.”
0.101	There is a lot of truth in this. It is certainly true that “we are saved by faith.” So it is also true that “whoever believes will be saved, and whoever does not believe will be damned.”
0.102	4. But some people will say with the apostle James, “Show me your faith without your works” (if you can—but really it is impossible), “and I will show you my faith by my works.”
0.103	And many are led to think that good works—works of devotion and of mercy—matter far more than faith itself. They think good works will make up for anything else a person lacks to be ready for heaven.
0.104	In fact, this seems to be what most people believe. Not only members of the Church of Rome think this, but Protestants too. And not just careless, thoughtless people, but serious members of our own Church.
0.105	5. And no one can deny this. Our Lord himself said, “You will know them by their fruits.” By their works you can tell those who believe from those who do not.
0.106	But we may still ask whether there is a surer proof that our faith is sincere than even our works. That proof is being willing to suffer for doing what is right. This is especially true if a person suffers insults and pain and the loss of friends and possessions, and then gives up life itself. He might even die a shameful and painful death, giving his body to be burned, rather than give up his faith and a good conscience by neglecting a duty he knows he has.
0.107	6. At this point we should notice, first, what a beautiful ladder this is, with each step rising above the last. It is the list of things that various people called Christians—and usually thought of as Christians—really believe can make up for not having love.
0.108	Paul begins at the lowest point, speaking well. Then he moves up step by step, each one higher than the one before, until he reaches the highest of all.
0.109	One step above eloquence is knowledge. Faith is one step above that.
0.110	Good works are one step above that faith. And even above good works is suffering for doing what is right.
0.111	Nothing is higher than this except Christian love—love for our neighbour that flows from love for God.
0.112	7. Second, we should notice something else. Whatever passes for religion anywhere in the Christian world can very easily be placed under one or another of these headings. That is true whether it really is part of religion or no part of it at all—just foolishness, superstition, or wickedness.
0.113	Protestants and Roman Catholics may think something is religion when it is not. Every such thing fits under one or another of these five points.
0.114	Test this as often as you like with anything that is wrongly called religion. You will find that the rule holds with no exceptions.
0.115	III. 1. Now, third, I want to prove something to everyone who has ears to hear and who does not harden himself against being convinced. None of these five things, and not even all of them together, will be worth anything before God without the love described above.
0.116	To do this as clearly as possible, we can look at them one at a time.
0.117	First: “If I speak in the languages of people and of angels.” Suppose I speak about the nature, qualities, and works of God, in creation or in providence, with an eloquence that no human has ever had. Suppose I am not the least bit behind the greatest apostles, preaching like Peter and praying like John. Even so, unless humble, gentle, patient love rules my soul, I am no better in God’s judgment “than a booming piece of brass or a rumbling cymbal.”
0.118	So the greatest eloquence, whether in private conversation or in public ministry—the most brilliant gifts for preaching or prayer—might sink me deeper into hell if they were not joined with a humble, gentle, and patient acceptance of God’s will. But they will not bring me one step closer to heaven.
0.119	2. A simple example may show this.
0.120	Between fifty and sixty years ago I knew a young man who, for several years, never tried to convince anyone of a religious truth without that person being convinced. And he never tried to persuade anyone to take up a religious practice without that person being persuaded. So what?
0.121	All that power to convince, all that force of persuasion, would not make him any more fit to enjoy God than a clear voice or a good complexion would—if it was not joined with gentleness and humility, with acceptance of God’s will and patient love.
0.122	In fact, it would rather earn him a hotter place in the everlasting flames!
0.123	3. Second: “If I have the gift of prophecy”—the gift of foretelling future events that no creature can foresee—and “if I understand all” the “mysteries” of nature, of providence, and of God’s word, and “have all knowledge” of things divine or human that any human being has ever reached. Suppose I can explain the most mysterious passages of Daniel, Ezekiel, and Revelation. Even so, if I do not have humility, gentleness, and acceptance of God’s will, “I am nothing” in God’s sight.
0.124	A little before the end of the recent war in Flanders, someone who came from there told us a very strange story.
0.125	I did not know what to make of it. So I waited until John Haime came back. I could no more doubt his honesty than his good sense.
0.126	This is what he told us: “Jonathan Pyrah was a member of our Society in Flanders.
0.127	I knew him for several years, and I knew that no one could find fault with his character.
0.128	One day he was ordered to appear before the Board of General Officers.
0.129	One of them said, ‘What is this we are hearing about you?
0.130	We hear that you have become a prophet. We hear you are predicting the fall of the bloody house of Bourbon and the proud house of Austria.
0.131	We would be glad if you were a real prophet and your prophecies came true.
0.132	But what sign will you give us to convince us that you are a prophet and that your predictions will happen?’
0.133	He answered at once, ‘Gentlemen, I will give you a sign. Tomorrow at twelve o’clock you will have a storm with more thunder and lightning than you have ever had since you came to Flanders.
0.134	I will give you a second sign. None of you expects anything like this, and there is no sign of it now. But within three days you will fight a full battle with the French.
0.135	I will give you a third sign. I will be ordered to advance in the front line.
0.136	If I am a false prophet, I will be shot dead in the first volley. But if I am a true prophet, I will only be hit by a musket ball in the calf of my left leg.’
0.137	At twelve o’clock the next day there was thunder and lightning like nothing they had ever had before in Flanders.
0.138	On the third day, against everyone’s expectations, the great battle of Fontenoy took place.
0.139	He was ordered to advance in the front line. And in the very first volley, he was indeed hit by a musket ball in the calf of his left leg.”
0.140	4. And yet none of this did him any good, either for happiness in this life or for eternal happiness.
0.141	When the war was over, he came back to England. But the story had got there before him. Because of this, the Countess of St—s and several other people of high rank sent for him. They wanted to hear such an amazing story from his own mouth.
0.142	He could not handle so much honour.
0.143	It completely turned his head.
0.144	Before long he went completely mad.
0.145	And he is still like that today. As far as I know, he is still living at Wibsey Moorside, a few miles from Leeds.
0.146	[This was so when this sermon was written.
0.147	He has since died.]
0.148	5. And what good would it do a person to “have all knowledge”—even the knowledge that is infinitely better than all other knowledge, the knowledge of the Holy Scripture?
0.149	About twenty years ago I knew a young man who knew the Bible extremely well. If someone asked him about any Hebrew word in the Old Testament or any Greek word in the New Testament, he would pause a moment. Then he would tell not only how often that word appeared in the Bible, but also what it meant in each place.
0.150	His name was Thomas Walsh.
0.151	[His journal, which he wrote himself, still exists.]
0.152	I had never seen such a master of Bible knowledge before, and I never expect to see one again.
0.153	But suppose that, with all his knowledge, he had no love. Suppose he had been proud, hot-tempered, or impatient. Then he and all his knowledge would have perished together, as surely as he was ever born.
0.154	6. “And if I have all faith, so that I could move mountains.” Faith that can do this cannot come from empty imagination. It is not just a madman’s dream or a set of opinions. It must be a real work of God. Otherwise it could not have such an effect.
0.155	But if this faith does not work through love—if it does not produce holiness in every part of life, if it does not bring humility, gentleness, and acceptance of God’s will—it will do me no good.
0.156	This is as certain as any truth in all of God’s word.
0.157	Any faith that now exists, ever existed, or ever can exist, is not Christian faith if it is separated from tender goodwill toward every human being—friend or enemy, Christian, Jew, heretic, or pagan. It is not Christian faith if it is separated from gentleness toward all people, from acceptance of whatever happens, and from contentment in every situation. Such faith will be of no use to us when we stand before God.
0.158	7. Listen to this, all of you who are called Methodists!
0.159	This concerns you more than anyone else alive.
0.160	You always talk about salvation by faith. And you are right to do so.
0.161	All of you, every one, hold that a person is justified by faith, without the works of the law.
0.162	And you cannot do anything else without giving up the Bible and betraying your own souls.
0.163	You insist that we are saved by faith. And we certainly are.
0.164	But at the same time, remember this. No matter how much faith we have, and no matter how strong it is, it will never save us from hell unless it saves us now from every unholy attitude. It must save us from pride, hot temper, and impatience; from all arrogance, all haughtiness and bossiness; from rage, anger, and bitterness; from discontent, grumbling, fretting, and irritability.
0.165	We have been warned so often against that powerful delusion. So we would have less excuse than anyone if we still give in to any of these attitudes, and at the same time congratulate ourselves and dream that we are on the way to heaven!
0.166	8. Fourth: “If I give all my goods to the poor.” Suppose I divide all my land and all my belongings into small portions (that is what the original word properly means). Suppose I carefully give them to the people I have good reason to believe most deserve them. Even so, if I am proud, hot-tempered, or discontented—if I give in to any of these attitudes—then whatever good I may do for others, I do none for my own soul.
0.167	Oh, how sad this is!
0.168	Who would not grieve that these generous people should lose all their work!
0.169	It is true that many of them get a reward in this world—if not before their death, then after it.
0.170	They have expensive and grand funerals.
0.171	They have marble monuments made with the finest craftsmanship.
0.172	They have epitaphs written in the most elegant style, praising their virtues to the skies.
0.173	Perhaps speeches are given over them every year, to pass their memory on to every generation.
0.174	Many founders of religious houses, colleges, almshouses, and most charitable institutions have all this too.
0.175	And it is an accepted rule that no one can praise the founder of his house, college, or hospital too much.
0.176	But even so, what a poor reward this is!
0.177	Suppose they are in the hands of the devil and his angels—which must be the case if they did not die in faith. Will all this add to their comfort or to their misery?
0.178	What insults, what cutting scorn, it would bring them from their companions in hell!
0.179	Oh, if only they were wise! If only all who are eager to do good works would put those works in their proper place! They should not imagine that good works can make up for a lack of holy attitudes. Instead they should make sure that their works grow out of those attitudes.
0.180	9. How very strange this must sound to most of the people whom English politeness calls Christians!
0.181	But even stranger is the apostle’s last statement: “If I give my body to be burned, and do not have love, it does me no good.”
0.182	Suppose I willingly accept a cruel death—“hand over my body to be burned”—rather than deny the faith, commit a known sin, or neglect a known duty. Even so, if pride or anger or fretfulness has power over me, “it does me no good.”
0.183	10. Perhaps an example will help show this.
0.184	There is a remarkable story in the writings of Dr. Geddes. He was an expert in civil law and served as Queen Anne’s envoy to the Court of Portugal near the end of her reign.
0.185	He was present at one of those Autos de Fé, or “Acts of Faith,” where the Roman Inquisitors burned heretics alive.
0.186	One of the people brought out to be executed had been locked in the dungeons of the Inquisition. He had not seen the sun for many years.
0.187	It turned out to be a bright, sunny day.
0.188	He looked up and cried out in surprise, “Oh, how can anyone who sees that glorious light worship anyone but the God who made it!”
0.189	A friar standing nearby ordered them to push an iron gag through his lips so that he could not speak any more.
0.190	Now, what did that poor man feel inside when this order was carried out?
0.191	He could not say anything with his lips. But suppose he said in his heart, “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.” Then the angels of God were certainly ready to carry his soul to Abraham’s side.
0.192	But suppose instead he held on to resentment in his heart that he could not express with his tongue. Then, even though his body was burned up in the flames, I will not say his soul went to paradise.
0.193	11. Here is the sum of all we have seen. Whatever I say, whatever I know, whatever I believe, whatever I do, whatever I suffer—if I do not have the faith that works through love, the faith that produces love for God and for all people, then I am not on the narrow road that leads to life. I am on the broad road that leads to destruction.
0.194	In other words: whatever eloquence I have; whatever natural or supernatural knowledge; whatever faith I have received from God; whatever works I do, whether of devotion or of mercy; whatever I suffer for conscience’ sake, even if I resist to the point of shedding blood—all these things together, however much people praise them, will be worth nothing before God. They are worth nothing unless I am gentle and humble in heart, and can say in everything, “Not what I want, but what you want!”
0.195	12. From all this we conclude—and this can never be stressed too much, because the whole world votes the other way—that true religion, at its very heart, is nothing less than holy attitudes.
0.196	So all other religion, whatever name it has—pagan, Muslim, Jewish, or Christian; Roman Catholic or Protestant; Lutheran or Reformed—is lighter than emptiness itself without these.
0.197	13. So everyone who has a soul to be saved must make sure of this one thing.
0.198	Along with all his eloquence, knowledge, faith, works, and sufferings, he must hold on to this “one thing that is needed.”
0.199	Whoever, through the power of faith, endures to the end in humble, gentle, patient love—that person, and that person alone, will, through the merits of Christ, “inherit the kingdom prepared from the foundation of the world.”

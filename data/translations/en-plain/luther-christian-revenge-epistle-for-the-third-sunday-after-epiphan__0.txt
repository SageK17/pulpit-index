0.9	"Do not be wise in your own opinions."
0.10	64. The reading used in church ends here.
0.11	So we will only look briefly at the rest.
0.12	"Your own opinions" here means a stubborn attitude about earthly things. It is the attitude of a person who does not want to be taught. He thinks he knows best about everything. He gives in to no one, and he calls good whatever fits his own ideas.
0.13	A Christian should be more willing to give in on earthly matters.
0.14	He should not be quarrelsome but ready to give way. After all, God's Word and faith are not at stake here. It is only a matter of personal honor, of friends, and of worldly things.
0.15	"Do not pay anyone back evil for evil."
0.16	65. Earlier (verse 14) the writer of the letter said, "Do not curse." There he was thinking of people who cannot take revenge or pay back evil for evil.
0.17	These people have no other choice but to curse, to call down evil on those who oppress them.
0.18	But here he is talking about people who have equal power to pay each other back evil for evil and spite for spite. They can do this by what they do or by what they fail to do. And usually it is by what they fail to do.
0.19	But a Christian should pay back good for evil, and not fail to do it.
0.20	God lets his sun shine on the evil and on the good.
0.21	Matthew 5:45.
0.22	"Be careful to do what is honorable in the eyes of everyone."
0.23	66. This command is like the one he gives the Thessalonians (1 Thess 5:22): "Stay away from every appearance of evil." It is also like what he tells the Philippians (4:8): "Whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is well spoken of. If there is any virtue and if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things."
0.24	This is only about how we act outwardly.
0.25	Paul does not want Christians to think they are free to do whatever they please, without caring whether others approve.
0.26	Only in matters of faith does a Christian have that freedom.
0.27	His outward behavior should be above blame and acceptable to everyone. This fits the teaching of 1 Corinthians 10:32-33: we should please everyone and give offense to neither Jews nor Gentiles. It also follows Peter's advice (1 Pet 2:12): "Keep your behavior proper among the Gentiles."
0.28	"If it is possible, as far as it depends on you, live at peace with everyone."
0.29	67. Here Paul means outward peace among people. This is peace with Christians and pagans, with the godly and the wicked, with people of high and low rank.
0.30	We must not give anyone a reason to fight. Instead, we should patiently put up with every evil. We should never let peace be broken because of us.
0.31	We must not pay back evil for evil or blow for blow. Whoever does that starts quarrels.
0.32	Paul adds, "As far as it depends on you."
0.33	We should avoid hurting anyone, so that we are not the ones who start a quarrel.
0.34	We must be friendly to everyone, even if they are not friendly to us.
0.35	It is impossible to keep the peace all the time.
0.36	As the saying goes, "I can only live in peace as long as my neighbor wants to."
0.37	But it is in our power to leave others in peace, both friends and enemies, and to put up with everyone's quarrels.
0.38	"Oh yes," you say, "but then where would we be?" Listen:
0.39	"Dear friends, do not take revenge yourselves. Instead, make room for the wrath of God. For it is written: Vengeance belongs to me; I will pay back, says the Lord."
0.40	68. Notice this: the apostle forbids us to pay back blow for blow or to take revenge. In this way he shows that if we want to enjoy peace, we must quietly put up with the trouble others cause us.
0.41	He does not only assure us that we will be avenged. He also frightens us away from taking over God's office, for vengeance and payback belong to God alone.
0.42	In fact, he rather grieves over what will happen to the Christian's enemies, who put themselves in the path of God's wrath. He wants to move us to pity them, because we must make room for wrath and let them fall into God's hands.
0.43	God's vengeance and wrath come in many ways. They come through the government and through the devil. They come by sickness, hunger, and plague, by fire and water, and by war, hostility, and disgrace. And they come by every kind of misfortune on earth.
0.44	Any creature can serve as God's rod and weapon when he means to punish.
0.45	As the Wisdom of Solomon says (5:17): "He shall ... make the creature his weapon to take revenge on his enemies."
0.46	69. So Paul says, "Make room for wrath."
0.47	I have added the words "of God" to make the meaning of the text clearer. The wrath of God is meant, not human wrath.
0.48	The idea is not that we should make room for the anger of our enemies.
0.49	It is true that there may be times for that too. But here Paul is not talking about human anger.
0.50	Clearly he means misfortunes and plagues, which are seen as expressions of God's wrath.
0.51	Maybe the apostle left out that phrase so that no one would think he meant only God's final wrath. That is his anger on the last day, when he will punish without using any other means.
0.52	Paul wants to include here all the wrath that God shows in his punishments, whether in this life or forever.
0.53	This is a way of speaking from the Old Testament.
0.54	Phinehas says (Josh 22:18), "Tomorrow he will be angry with ...
0.55	Israel." And in several places Moses speaks of God's anger flaring up.
0.56	See Numbers 11:1, 10, 33.
0.57	I mention these things to teach this: when the government uses the sword to punish its enemies, we should see it as an expression of God's wrath. And the words in Deuteronomy 32:35, "Vengeance is mine," do not refer only to punishment that God brings directly, without any other means.
0.58	"But if your enemy is hungry, feed him. If he is thirsty, give him something to drink. For by doing this you will heap coals of fire on his head."
0.59	70. This teaching supports what I have already said: the Christian's enemies deserve pity, because they are under the wrath of God.
0.60	So it is not Christian to harm them. Instead, we should do them favors.
0.61	Here Paul quotes Solomon.
0.62	Proverbs 25:21-22. I think heaping coals of fire on someone's head means doing favors for the enemy.
0.63	Our kindness sets him on fire. In the end he becomes unhappy with himself and friendlier toward us.
0.64	Here the coals are kindnesses, or favors.
0.65	In the same way, the coals in the incense burner stand for God's favors, or blessings. They are a picture of our prayers, which should rise up with fervor.
0.66	Some say that the coals stand for God's Law and judgments (see Psalm 18:8, "Coals were kindled by it"). They reason that the Christian's kindness forces his enemy to blame himself and to feel the weight of God's Law and his judgments.
0.67	I do not think a Christian should want punishment to fall on his enemy. Still, this explanation of the verse is not a bad one.
0.68	In fact, it fits well with the command "Make room for wrath." That means: do good, and then wrath—the coals—will easily fall on the enemy.
0.69	"Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good."
0.70	71. It seems to me that with this final counsel Paul himself explains the words "coals of fire." He explains them in line with the first idea: an enemy's malice is to be overcome with good.
0.71	Overcoming by force is the same as giving yourself over to evil and wronging the enemy who wrongs you.
0.72	If you do that, your enemy overcomes you, and you become evil like him.
0.73	But if you overcome him with good, he will become righteous like you.
0.74	Here a spiritual victory is meant. The attitude, the heart, and the soul are overcome—yes, even the devil who stirs up the evil.

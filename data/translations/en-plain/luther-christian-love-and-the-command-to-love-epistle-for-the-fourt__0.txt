0.258	“Take these commands: Do not commit adultery. Do not kill. Do not steal. Do not covet. If there is any other command, it is summed up in short in this one word: Love your neighbor as yourself.”
0.259	29. Love is the main thing in every law. So, as we have shown clearly enough, it includes all the commandments.
0.260	Its only concern is to help people and not to hurt them. That is why it easily finds the way.
0.261	Love sees that people, because they love themselves so much, try to look after their own interests and avoid anything that would harm them. So love tries to do the same for others.
0.262	Let us look at the commandment we just quoted, and notice how cleverly and wisely it is put together.
0.263	It brings out four ideas.
0.264	First, it tells us who has the duty to love: you. That is the nearest, noblest and best person any of us can command.
0.265	No one can keep God’s Law for someone else. Each person must do it for himself.
0.266	As Paul says (Galatians 6:5), “Each person will carry his own burden.”
0.267	And (2 Corinthians 5:10): “All of us must be shown for what we are before the judgment seat of Christ. Then each one will receive back what he did while in the body, according to what he has done, whether good or bad.”
0.268	So it says, “You, you yourself, must love.” It does not say, “Let someone else love for you.”
0.269	We can and should pray that God will be kind to someone else and help him. But no one will be saved unless he himself keeps God’s command.
0.270	It is not enough just to pray that someone will escape punishment, as the people who sell indulgences teach. Much more, we should pray that he will become righteous and obey God’s commands.
0.271	30. Second, the commandment names the noblest virtue of all: love.
0.272	It does not say, “Feed your neighbor, give him something to drink, give him clothes,” even though all of these are wonderfully good works. It says, “Love him.”
0.273	Love is the greatest virtue, the spring that all the other virtues flow from.
0.274	Love gives food and drink. It clothes people, comforts them, urges them on, relieves them and rescues them.
0.275	What can we say about it? Look: the one who loves gives himself, body and soul, his belongings and his honor, all his powers inside and out, for the good of his needy neighbor, whether that neighbor is a friend or an enemy. He holds back nothing that he could use to serve someone else.
0.276	No virtue is like love. You cannot give it just one special job, the way you can with smaller virtues like purity, mercy, patience, gentleness and so on.
0.277	Love does everything.
0.278	It will suffer in life and in death, in every situation, and it will do this even for its enemies.
0.279	No wonder Paul says here that all the other commandments are summed up in short in this command: “Love your neighbor as yourself.”
0.280	31. Third, the commandment names the place where our love is to work. It is the noblest field and the dearest friend: our neighbor.
0.281	It does not say, “Love the rich, the powerful, the educated, the saint.”
0.282	No, the boundless love named in this most perfect commandment does not share itself out among just a few people.
0.283	It does not play favorites.
0.284	False, selfish, worldly love does play favorites. It loves someone only as long as it hopes to get something out of it.
0.285	When that hope ends, that love ends too.
0.286	But the commandment in our text asks us for a love that is free and willing. It is for all people, whoever they are, friend or enemy. It is a love that does not look for gain, and gives only what is good for others.

0.289	This commandment is for everyone, for every human being. So a king, if he is a human being, must admit that the poorest beggar and the most miserable leper is his neighbor and his equal in God’s eyes.

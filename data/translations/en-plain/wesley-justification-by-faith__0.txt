0.1	“Suppose someone does not work, but believes in the one who justifies ungodly people. That person’s faith is counted as righteousness.” Romans 4:5.
0.2	1. How can a sinner be justified before God, the Lord and Judge of all? For every human being, this is a question of great importance.
0.3	It holds the foundation of all our hope. For as long as we are enemies of God, we can have no true peace and no lasting joy, either in this life or in eternity.
0.4	What peace can we have while our own heart condemns us? And even more, while he who is “greater than our heart and knows everything” condemns us?
0.5	What lasting joy can we have, in this world or in the world to come, while “God’s anger remains on us”?
0.6	2. And yet people have understood this important question so poorly!
0.7	Many have had such confused ideas about it!
0.8	In fact, these ideas have been not only confused but often completely false. They are as opposite to the truth as light is to darkness. They do not fit at all with God’s own words or with the whole pattern of the faith.
0.9	So they went wrong about the very foundation, and they could not build on it. At least, they could not build with “gold, silver, or precious stones,” which would last when tested by fire. They could only build with “hay and straw,” which neither pleases God nor helps people.
0.10	3. This subject is hugely important, and I want to do it justice as far as I can. I want to save sincere seekers of the truth from “empty talk and arguments about words.” I want to clear up the confusion that so many have already been led into by these things. And I want to give them a true and right understanding of this great mystery of godliness. So I will try to show:
0.11	First, what the general basis is of this whole teaching about justification.
0.12	Second, what justification is.
0.13	Third, who the people are that are justified. And,
0.14	Fourth, on what terms they are justified.
0.15	I. First, then, I will show what the general basis is of this whole teaching about justification.
0.16	1. Man was made in the image of God. He was holy, just as his Creator is holy. He was merciful, just as the Maker of all things is merciful. He was perfect, just as his Father in heaven is perfect.
0.17	God is love. So man lived in love, and in this way he lived in God, and God lived in him.
0.18	God made him to be an “image of his own eternity,” a picture of the God of glory that could not decay.
0.19	So man was pure, just as God is pure, without any stain of sin.
0.20	He knew no evil of any kind or in any degree. Inside and outside, he was without sin and without stain.
0.21	He “loved the Lord his God with all his heart, and with all his mind, soul, and strength.”
0.22	2. Man was upright and perfect. So God gave him a perfect law, and he required full and perfect obedience to it.
0.23	God required full obedience in every point. And man had to obey without any break, from the moment he became a living soul until his time of testing was over.
0.24	No allowance was made for falling short in any way. In fact, none was needed. Man was fully able to do the task he was given, and he was completely equipped for every good word and deed.
0.25	3. The whole law of love was written in man’s heart. (Perhaps he could not sin against it directly.) But in his sovereign wisdom, God chose to add one specific command to it: “You must not eat the fruit of the tree that grows in the middle of the garden.” And God attached a penalty to it: “On the day you eat from it, you will surely die.”
0.26	4. So this was man’s condition in Paradise.
0.27	Through God’s free love, which man had not earned, he was holy and happy. He knew God, loved God, and enjoyed God. And that, in essence, is eternal life.
0.28	He would continue in this life of love forever, as long as he kept obeying God in everything. But if he disobeyed God in anything, he would lose it all.
0.29	God said, “On that day you will surely die.”
0.30	5. Man did disobey God.
0.31	He “ate from the tree that God had commanded him about, saying, You must not eat from it.”
0.32	And on that day God’s righteous judgment condemned him.
0.33	Then the sentence he had been warned about also began to take effect in him.
0.34	For the moment he tasted that fruit, he died.
0.35	His soul died. It was cut off from God. And a soul cut off from God has no more life than a body cut off from its soul.
0.36	His body also became subject to decay and death. So death took hold of his body too.
0.37	He was already dead in spirit: dead to God and dead in sin. And he was rushing on toward eternal death, toward the destruction of body and soul in the fire that never goes out.
0.38	6. So “sin entered the world through one man, and death came through sin.
0.39	And so death spread to all people,” because we were all contained in him. He was the common father and representative of us all.
0.40	So “through the offense of one man,” all are dead. They are dead to God and dead in sin. They live in a body that decays and dies and will soon fall apart. And they are under the sentence of eternal death.
0.41	For “by one man’s disobedience” all “were made sinners.” In the same way, through that one man’s offense, “judgment came on all people and condemned them.”
0.42	(Romans 5:12)
0.43	7. All of humanity was in this state when “God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, so that we would not perish but have eternal life.”
0.44	At the right time, he became a man. He became another common head of humanity, a second parent and representative of the whole human race.
0.45	And in that role “he carried our sorrows,” as “the Lord laid on him the sins of us all.”
0.46	Then he was “wounded for our wrongdoing and bruised for our sins.”
0.47	“He made his soul an offering for sin.” He poured out his blood for those who broke God’s law. He “carried our sins in his own body on the cross,” so that by his wounds we might be healed. And by that one offering of himself, made once, he has redeemed me and all people. By doing this he “made a full, perfect, and sufficient sacrifice and payment for the sins of the whole world.”
0.48	8. The Son of God has “tasted death for everyone.” Because of this, God has now “brought the world back to himself, not counting their” past “sins against them.”
0.49	And so, “just as one man’s offense brought judgment and condemnation on all people, so one man’s righteousness brought the free gift to all people, so that they can be justified.”
0.50	So now, for the sake of his dearly loved Son and what he has done and suffered for us, God graciously offers to cancel the punishment our sins deserve. He offers to bring us back into his favor. And he offers to bring our dead souls back to spiritual life, as a down payment on eternal life. He does this on only one condition, and he himself also makes us able to meet it.
0.51	9. So this is the general basis of the whole teaching about justification.
0.52	Adam was not only the father of us all but also our representative. Through the sin of this first Adam, we all lost God’s favor. We all became children of wrath. Or, as the apostle puts it, “judgment came on all people and condemned them.”
0.53	In the same way, the Second Adam, as the representative of us all, made a sacrifice for sin. Because of it, God is reconciled to the whole world so far that he has given them a new covenant. Its condition is simple. Once it is met, “there is no more condemnation” for us. Instead, “we are justified freely by his grace, through the redemption that is in Jesus Christ.”
0.54	II. 1. But what does it mean to be “justified”?
0.55	What is “justification”? This was the second thing I planned to show.
0.56	From what we have already seen, it is clear that it does not mean actually being made just and righteous.
0.57	That is “sanctification.” In some degree, sanctification is the direct fruit of justification. But it is still a separate gift of God, and it is completely different in nature.
0.58	Justification is what God does for us through his Son. Sanctification is what he works in us by his Spirit.
0.59	In a few rare cases, the word “justified” or “justification” is used in such a broad sense that it includes “sanctification” too. But in general use, St. Paul and the other inspired writers keep the two clearly apart.
0.60	2. Some have a far-fetched idea that justification means clearing us from accusation, especially the accusation of Satan. But this is not easy to prove from any clear text of Holy Scripture.
0.61	In the whole account that Scripture gives of this matter, as I described it above, neither that accuser nor his accusation seems to play any part at all.
0.62	Of course, no one can deny that he is the “accuser” of human beings. That is the very name given to him.
0.63	But there is no sign at all that the great apostle refers to this in any way in everything he wrote about justification, either to the Romans or to the Galatians.
0.64	3. Some also claim that justification means clearing us from the accusation the law brings against us. This is much easier to assume than to prove from any clear statement of Scripture. At least, that is so if this forced, unnatural way of speaking means anything more or less than the following. We have broken God’s law and so deserve to be damned in hell. But God does not give those who are justified the punishment they deserved.
0.65	4. Least of all does justification mean that God is fooled about the people he justifies. It does not mean that he thinks they are something they really are not, or that he regards them as different from what they are.
0.66	It certainly does not mean that God judges us in a way that goes against the real facts. He does not think we are better than we really are. He does not believe we are righteous when we are unrighteous.
0.67	Surely not! The judgment of the all-wise God always agrees with the truth.
0.68	And his perfect wisdom could never think that I am innocent, or judge that I am righteous or holy, just because someone else is.
0.69	He can no more mix me up with Christ in this way than he could mix me up with David or Abraham.
0.70	Let anyone to whom God has given understanding weigh this without bias. He will surely see that this idea of justification fits neither with reason nor with Scripture.
0.71	5. The plain meaning of justification in Scripture is pardon, the forgiveness of sins.
0.72	It is the act of God the Father by which he “shows his righteousness (or mercy) by forgiving the sins of the past.” He does this for the sake of the atoning sacrifice made by the blood of his Son.
0.73	This is the simple, natural explanation St. Paul gives of it throughout this whole letter.
0.74	He explains it this way himself, especially in this chapter and the next.
0.75	So, two verses after our text, he says, “Blessed are those whose wrongs are forgiven and whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man whose sin the Lord will not count against him.”
0.76	For the person who is justified or forgiven, God “will not count sin” against him to condemn him.
0.77	God will not condemn him for it, either in this world or in the world to come.
0.78	His sins, all his past sins in thought, word, and deed, are covered and wiped out. They will not be remembered or mentioned against him, just as if they had never been.
0.79	God will not make that sinner suffer what he deserved, because God’s beloved Son has suffered for him.
0.80	From the moment we are “accepted through the Beloved” and “reconciled to God through his blood,” God loves us, blesses us, and watches over us for our good, just as if we had never sinned.
0.81	It is true that in one place the apostle seems to stretch the meaning of the word much further. He says, “It is not those who hear the law, but those who do the law, who will be justified.”
0.82	Here he seems to connect our justification with the verdict of the great day.
0.83	Our Lord himself certainly does this when he says, “By your words you will be justified.” He shows by this that “people will give an account on the day of judgment for every careless word they speak.”
0.84	But perhaps we can hardly find another example of St. Paul using the word in that distant sense.
0.85	In his writings in general, he clearly does not. And least of all does he do so in the text we are looking at. It clearly speaks not of those who have already “finished their course,” but of those who are only now “setting out,” who are just beginning to “run the race set before them.”
0.86	III. 1. This brings us to the third thing we were to consider: Who are the people that are justified?
0.87	The apostle tells us plainly: the ungodly. “He (that is, God) justifies the ungodly.” He justifies ungodly people of every kind and degree, and no one but the ungodly.
0.88	“Those who are righteous need no repentance.” In the same way, they need no forgiveness.
0.89	Only sinners have any need of pardon. Only sin can be forgiven.
0.90	So forgiveness has to do directly with sin, and in this respect with nothing else.
0.91	It is our “unrighteousness” that the forgiving God is “merciful” to. It is our “wrongdoing” that he “remembers no more.”
0.92	2. Some people argue very strongly that a person must be sanctified, that is, holy, before he can be justified. Some of them even claim that complete holiness or obedience must come before justification. They do not seem to have considered this at all.
0.93	(Unless they mean justification at the last day, which is a completely different question.)
0.94	Far from it! The very idea is not only flatly impossible, but also grossly and deeply absurd, because it contradicts itself. (It is impossible because where there is no love for God, there is no holiness. And there is no love for God except from knowing that he loves us.)
0.95	For it is not a saint who is forgiven, but a sinner, and he is forgiven as a sinner.
0.96	God does not justify the godly but the ungodly. He does not justify those who are already holy, but those who are unholy.
0.97	We will soon look at the condition on which he does this. But whatever it is, it cannot be holiness.
0.98	To claim that it is holiness would be to say that the Lamb of God takes away only the sins that were already taken away.
0.99	3. Does the good Shepherd, then, search for and save only those who have already been found?
0.100	No! He searches for and saves what is lost.
0.101	He forgives those who need his forgiving mercy.
0.102	He saves sinners of every kind and every degree from the guilt of sin (and at the same time from its power). Until then, these people were completely ungodly. The Father’s love was not in them. So nothing good lived in them, no good or truly Christian attitude. Instead, all their attitudes were evil and hateful: pride, anger, love of the world. These are the true fruits of the “fleshly mind,” which is “at war with God.”
0.103	4. It is the sick who need a Doctor: those who find the burden of their sins unbearable. It is the guilty who need a pardon: those who groan under God’s anger.
0.104	Some people are “condemned already” for all their ungodliness in thought, word, and deed. They are condemned not only by God but also by their own conscience, which is like a thousand witnesses. These people cry out loudly for him who “justifies the ungodly” through the redemption that is in Jesus. He justifies the ungodly, and “the one who does not work.” Before he is justified, such a person does nothing good, nothing truly virtuous or holy. He only does evil all the time.
0.105	For his heart is necessarily and essentially evil until the love of God is poured into it.
0.106	And while the tree is rotten, so is its fruit. For “a bad tree cannot produce good fruit.”
0.107	5. Someone may object: “But a person who is not yet justified can feed the hungry or clothe the naked. And these are good works.” The answer is easy. Yes, he can do these things even before he is justified. And in one sense these are “good works.” They are “good and helpful to people.”
0.108	But that does not mean that, strictly speaking, they are good in themselves, or good in God’s eyes.
0.109	All truly “good works” (to use the words of our Church) “come after justification.” And they are good and “pleasing to God in Christ” for this reason: they “grow out of a true and living faith.”
0.110	By the same reasoning, all “works done before justification are not good” in the Christian sense, “because they do not grow out of faith in Jesus Christ.” (They may grow out of some kind of faith in God.) “In fact, because they are not done as God has willed and commanded them to be done, we have no doubt” (however strange this may seem to some) “that they have the nature of sin.”
0.111	6. Perhaps those who doubt this have not thought carefully enough about the weighty reason given here. It explains why no works done before justification can be truly and properly good.
0.112	The argument clearly goes like this:
0.113	No works are good unless they are done as God has willed and commanded them to be done.
0.114	But no works done before justification are done as God has willed and commanded them to be done.
0.115	So no works done before justification are good.
0.116	The first statement is obvious. The second is that no works done before justification are done as God has willed and commanded them to be done. This will seem just as plain and undeniable if we simply consider this: God has willed and commanded that “all our works” should “be done in charity” (en agape), that is, in love. That means in the love for God that produces love for all people.
0.117	But none of our works can be done in this love while the love of the Father (of God as our Father) is not in us. And this love cannot be in us until we receive the Spirit of adoption, who cries out in our hearts, Abba, Father.
0.118	So if God does not justify the ungodly, and the one who (in this sense) does not work, then Christ died for nothing. Then, in spite of his death, no living person could be justified.
0.119	IV. 1. On what terms, then, is a person justified who is completely “ungodly” and who until then “does not work”? On one term only, and that is faith. He “believes in him who justifies the ungodly.”
0.120	And “whoever believes is not condemned.” In fact, he “has passed from death to life.”
0.121	“For the righteousness (or mercy) of God comes by faith in Jesus Christ to all and upon all who believe. God set him forth as a sacrifice of atonement, through faith in his blood, so that he might be just and” (in keeping with his justice) “the one who justifies the person who believes in Jesus.” “So we conclude that a person is justified by faith without the deeds of the law.” That is, without first obeying the moral law, which in fact he could not do until now.
0.122	The words that follow clearly show that the moral law, and only the moral law, is meant here: “Do we then cancel the law through faith?
0.123	Of course not! In fact, we establish the law.
0.124	What law do we establish by faith?
0.125	Not the ritual law, not the ceremonial law of Moses.
0.126	Not at all. We establish the great, unchanging law of love: holy love for God and for our neighbor.”
0.127	2. Faith in general is a divine, supernatural “elegchos”: an “evidence” or “conviction” “of things not seen.” These are things our bodily senses cannot detect, because they are past, future, or spiritual.
0.128	Justifying faith includes a divine evidence or conviction that “God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself.” But it also includes a sure trust and confidence that Christ died for “my” sins, that he loved “me” and gave himself for “me.”
0.129	Whenever a sinner believes like this, whether as a young child, in the prime of life, or when old and gray, God justifies that ungodly person. For the sake of his Son, God pardons him and sets him free, though until then there was nothing good in him.
0.130	It is true that God had given him repentance before this. But that repentance was nothing more or less than a deep awareness that he lacked every good thing and that every evil was present in him.
0.131	And whatever good he has or does from the hour he first believes in God through Christ, faith does not “find” it there but “brings” it.
0.132	This is the fruit of faith.
0.133	First the tree is good, and then the fruit is good too.
0.134	3. I cannot describe the nature of this faith better than in the words of our own Church: “The only means of salvation” (and justification is one part of salvation) “is faith. That is, it is a sure trust and confidence that God has forgiven our sins and will forgive them, and that he has accepted us back into his favor, because of the merits of Christ’s death and suffering. But here we must be careful not to falter with God through a faith that is unsteady and wavering. Peter came to Christ on the water, but because his faith grew weak, he was in danger of drowning. In the same way, if we begin to waver or doubt, we must fear that we will sink as Peter did, not into the water, but into the bottomless pit of hell fire.” (“Second Sermon on the Passion”)
0.135	“So have a sure and steady faith, not only that the death of Christ is effective for the whole world, but that he has made a full and sufficient sacrifice for “you,” a perfect cleansing of “your” sins. Then you can say with the apostle that he loved “you” and gave himself for “you.”
0.136	For this is how you make Christ “your own” and apply his merits to “yourself.” (“Sermon on the Sacrament, First Part”)
0.137	4. I say that this faith is the term or “condition of justification.” By this I mean, first, that there is no justification without it.
0.138	“Whoever does not believe is condemned already.” As long as he does not believe, that condemnation cannot be taken away. Instead, “God’s anger remains on him.”
0.139	“There is no other name given under heaven” than the name of Jesus of Nazareth. There is no other merit by which a condemned sinner can ever be saved from the guilt of sin. In the same way, there is no other way to receive a share in his merit than “by faith in his name.”
0.140	So as long as we are without this faith, we are “strangers to the covenant of promise.” We are “outsiders to the community of Israel, and without God in the world.”
0.141	A person may have all kinds of so-called virtues. (I am speaking of those to whom the gospel is preached. For “what business is it of mine to judge those outside?”) He may do all kinds of works that are considered good. But it does him no good. He is still a “child of wrath” and still under the curse, until he believes in Jesus.
0.142	5. So faith is the “necessary” condition of justification. In fact, it is the “only necessary” condition.
0.143	This is the second point we must carefully note. The very moment God gives faith (for “it is the gift of God”) to the “ungodly” person who “does not work,” that “faith is counted to him as righteousness.”
0.144	Before this he has no righteousness at all. He does not even have negative righteousness, that is, innocence.
0.145	But “faith is credited to him as righteousness” the very moment he believes.
0.146	This does not mean (as I said before) that God thinks he is something he is not.
0.147	But “God made Christ to be sin for us.” That is, he treated him as a sinner and punished him for our sins. In the same way, God counts us righteous from the time we believe in him. That is, he does not punish us for our sins. In fact, he treats us as though we were innocent and righteous.
0.148	6. Some find it hard to agree that “faith is the only condition of justification.” Surely that difficulty must come from not understanding it.
0.149	What we mean is this: it is the only thing without which no one is justified. It is the only thing that is directly, absolutely required for pardon, the one thing that cannot be done without.
0.150	On the one hand, a person might have everything else, but without faith he cannot be justified. On the other hand, a person might lack everything else, but if he has faith, he cannot fail to be justified.
0.151	For imagine a sinner of any kind or degree. He fully knows how completely ungodly he is. He knows he is totally unable to think, speak, or do good, and that he fully deserves hell-fire. Now imagine that this sinner, helpless and hopeless, throws himself completely on God’s mercy in Christ (which in fact he can only do by the grace of God). Who can doubt that he is forgiven in that moment?
0.152	Who will claim that anything more is “absolutely required” before that sinner can be justified?
0.153	Now, suppose there has been even one such case since the beginning of the world. (And have there not been, and are there not still, ten thousand times ten thousand?) Then it clearly follows that faith, in the sense given above, is the only condition of justification.
0.154	7. We are poor, guilty, sinful worms. We receive every blessing we enjoy by grace, as pure favor, not because it is owed to us. This is true from the smallest drop of water that cools our tongue to the immense riches of glory in eternity. So it is not fitting for us to ask God the reasons for what he does.
0.155	It is not right for us to question him “who gives no account of any of his ways.” It is not right to demand, “Why did you make faith the condition, the only condition, of justification?
0.156	Why did you decide that ‘whoever believes,’ and only he, ‘will be saved’?”
0.157	This is the very point St. Paul insists on so strongly in the ninth chapter of this letter. The terms of pardon and acceptance must depend not on us but “on him who calls us.” There is no “unrighteousness with God” when he sets his own terms, not according to ours but according to his own good pleasure. He may rightly say, “I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy,” that is, on the one who believes in Jesus.
0.158	“So then it does not depend on the one who wills, or on the one who runs,” to choose the condition on which he will be accepted, “but on God who shows mercy.” God accepts no one at all except out of his own free love, his undeserved goodness.
0.159	“So he has mercy on whom he wants to have mercy,” that is, on those who believe in the Son he loves. “And whom he wants,” that is, those who do not believe, “he hardens.” In the end he leaves them to the hardness of their hearts.
0.160	8. Still, we may humbly suggest one reason why God set this condition of justification: “If you believe in the Lord Jesus Christ, you will be saved.” It was to “keep pride away from man.”
0.161	Pride had already destroyed even angels of God. It had thrown down “a third of the stars of heaven.”
0.162	Pride was also largely the reason Adam fell from his firm position and brought sin and death into the world. This happened when the tempter said, “You will be like gods.”
0.163	So it was an act of wisdom worthy of God to set a condition of reconciliation for Adam and all his descendants that would truly humble them and bring them down to the dust.
0.164	And faith is such a condition.
0.165	It is especially suited for this purpose. For whoever comes to God by this faith must fix his eyes only on his own wickedness, his guilt, and his helplessness. He must not pay the least attention to any good he supposes is in himself, to any virtue or righteousness of any kind.
0.166	He must come as a “mere sinner,” inside and out, ruined and condemned by himself. He brings nothing to God but ungodliness. He pleads nothing of his own but sin and misery.
0.167	Only in this way, when his “mouth is shut” and he stands completely “guilty before” God, can he “look to Jesus” as the whole and only “atoning sacrifice for his sins.”
0.168	Only in this way can he be “found in him” and receive the “righteousness that comes from God by faith.”
0.169	9. You ungodly one, who hear or read these words! You vile, helpless, miserable sinner!
0.170	Before God, the Judge of all, I solemnly charge you: go straight to him, with all your ungodliness.
0.171	Be careful not to destroy your own soul by pleading your own righteousness, whether much or little.
0.172	Go as someone completely ungodly, guilty, lost, and ruined, deserving hell and falling into it. Then you will find favor in his sight, and you will know that he justifies the ungodly.
0.173	As such a person you will be brought to the “sprinkled blood,” as a ruined, helpless, damned sinner.
0.174	So “look to Jesus!”
0.175	There is “the Lamb of God,” who “takes away your sins!”
0.176	Do not plead any works, any righteousness of your own! Do not plead humility, sorrow for sin, or sincerity!
0.177	Not at all. That would truly be denying the Lord who bought you.
0.178	No! Plead only the blood of the covenant, the ransom paid for your proud, stubborn, sinful soul.
0.179	Who are you, who now see and feel your ungodliness, both inside and out?
0.180	You are the man!
0.181	I want you for my Lord!
0.182	I claim “you” as a child of God by faith!
0.183	The Lord needs you.
0.184	You who feel that you are just fit for hell are just fit to advance his glory: the glory of his free grace, which justifies the ungodly and the one who does not work.
0.185	Oh, come quickly! Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you, yes you, are reconciled to God.

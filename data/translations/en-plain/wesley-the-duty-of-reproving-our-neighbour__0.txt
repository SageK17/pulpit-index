0.1	“Do not hate your brother in your heart. Be sure to rebuke your neighbour, and do not let sin remain on him.” Leviticus 19:17.
0.2	A large part of the book of Exodus, and almost all of the book of Leviticus, deals with the ritual or ceremonial law of Moses. This law was given especially to the people of Israel. But the apostle Peter says it was “a yoke” that “neither our fathers nor we were able to carry.”
0.3	So we have been set free from it. This is one part of “the freedom with which Christ has set us free.”
0.4	Still, it is easy to see that many excellent moral commands are scattered among these ceremonial laws.
0.5	We find several of them in this very chapter. For example: “Do not gather every grape from your vineyard. Leave them for the poor and the stranger.
0.6	I am the Lord your God.”
0.7	(Leviticus 19:10.) Do not steal, and do not lie to one another.
0.8	(Leviticus 19:11.) “Do not cheat your neighbour or rob him. Do not keep a hired worker’s wages with you until the morning.”
0.9	(Leviticus 19:13.) “Do not curse the deaf, and do not put something in front of the blind to make them stumble. Fear your God instead. I am the Lord.”
0.10	(Leviticus 19:14.) It is as if he said: I am the one whose eyes watch over the whole earth, and whose ears are open to their cry.
0.11	“Do not be unjust when you judge. Do not favour the poor”—kind-hearted people may be tempted to do that—“and do not honour the powerful”—there are a thousand temptations to do that.
0.12	(Leviticus 19:15.) “Do not go around spreading gossip among your people.” (Leviticus 19:16.) Yet this is a sin that human laws have never been able to stop.
0.13	Then come these words: “Do not hate your brother in your heart. Be sure to rebuke your neighbour, and do not let sin remain on him.” (Leviticus 19:17.)
0.14	This is an important instruction. To understand it rightly, and to apply it to our own souls in a helpful way, let us look at these questions:
0.15	I. What is it that we must rebuke or correct?
0.16	What exactly is being commanded here?
0.17	II. Who are the people we are commanded to correct? And,
0.18	III. How should we correct them?
0.19	I. 1. First, let us ask: What is the duty that is commanded here?
0.20	What is it that we must rebuke or correct?
0.21	And what does it mean to correct someone?
0.22	What does it mean to correct?
0.23	It means telling a person about his faults. This is clear from the words that follow: “Do not let sin remain on him.”
0.24	So sin is the thing we are called to correct—or rather, the person who commits the sin.
0.25	We must do everything we can to convince him of his fault and lead him into the right way.
0.26	2. Love does require us to warn him about sin, and mainly about sin. But love also requires us to warn him about any error that would naturally lead to sin if he kept holding on to it.
0.27	If we do not “hate him in our heart,” and if we love our neighbour as ourselves, we will always try to do this. We will warn him about every evil way, and about every mistake that leads toward evil.
0.28	3. But if we do not want to waste our effort, we should seldom correct anyone about something that can be argued—something where much can be said on both sides.
0.29	Something may seem wrong to me, so I have doubts about doing it. And if I did it while I still had those doubts, I would be sinning before God.
0.30	But another person is not to be judged by my conscience. He stands or falls before his own master.
0.31	So I would not correct him except for what is clearly and undeniably wrong.
0.32	One example is cursing and swearing that dishonours God. Even people who do it most will seldom dare to defend it, if someone gently reasons with them.
0.33	Drunkenness is another. Even a habitual drunkard will condemn it when he is sober.
0.34	And most people would say the same about dishonouring the Lord’s day.
0.35	Some who are guilty of these sins may try to defend them for a while. But very few will keep it up if you look them steadily in the face and appeal to their own conscience before God.
0.36	II. 1. Second, let us ask: Who are the people we are called to correct?
0.37	This is all the more important to think about, because many serious people claim that there are some sinners whom the Bible itself forbids us to correct.
0.38	They take this meaning from our Lord’s solemn warning in his Sermon on the Mount: “Do not throw your pearls in front of pigs. If you do, they will trample them under their feet, and turn and tear you apart.”
0.39	But the plain meaning of these words is this: Do not offer the pearls—the great teachings or mysteries of the gospel—to men you know are like beasts. They are sunk in sin and have no fear of God before their eyes.
0.40	That would expose those precious jewels to scorn, and it would expose you to harm.
0.41	But think of people whom we know to be dogs and pigs in our Lord’s sense. If we see them do, or hear them say, something they themselves know is wrong, we must still correct them. Otherwise we “hate our brother in our heart.”
0.42	2. “Our neighbour” means every human being—everyone who breathes the air of life, everyone who has a soul to be saved.
0.43	Suppose we hold back from doing this loving service for some people because they are worse sinners than others. They may go on in their sin, but God will require their blood from our hands.
0.44	3. How striking are Mr. Baxter’s thoughts about this in his book “The Saints’ Everlasting Rest”!
0.45	“Suppose you met someone in the world below whom you had refused this loving service while you both lived under the sun. What answer could you give when he blamed you?
0.46	‘At such a time and place, while we lived under the sun, God put me into your hands.
0.47	Back then I did not know the way of salvation. I was chasing death, lost in the error of my life. And you left me like that, without even once trying to wake me out of my sleep!
0.48	If you had shared your knowledge with me, and warned me to flee from the coming wrath, neither of us would ever have had to come to this place of torment.’”
0.49	4. So everyone who has a soul to be saved has a right to this loving service from you.
0.50	But this does not mean that it must be given to everyone to the same degree.
0.51	No one can deny that it is owed especially to some people.
0.52	First of all, it is owed to our parents, if we have parents who need it—unless we put our husbands or wives and our children on the same level as them.
0.53	Next come our brothers and sisters. After them come our relatives, whether they are close or distant to us, by blood or by marriage.
0.54	Right after these come our servants, whether they are bound to us for a number of years or for a shorter time.
0.55	Last of all, each to their own degree, come the people of our country, our fellow citizens, and the members of the same society, whether civil or religious. Religious societies have a special claim on our service. They are formed for this very purpose: to watch over each other, so that we do not let sin remain on our brother.
0.56	If we fail to correct any of these people when a good chance comes, we certainly belong among those who “hate their brother in their heart.”
0.57	And how severe is the apostle’s sentence on those who come under this condemnation!
0.58	“Anyone who hates his brother”—even if the hatred never comes out in words or actions—“is a murderer. And you know,” the apostle goes on, “that no murderer has eternal life remaining in him.”
0.59	That seed which grows up into eternal life has not been planted in his soul. In other words, he is in such a state that if he dies in it, he cannot see life.
0.60	So it clearly follows that neglecting this is no small thing. It puts our final salvation in great danger.
0.61	III. We have seen what it means to correct our brother, and who we should correct.
0.62	But the most important thing still has to be considered.
0.63	How, in what way, should we correct them?
0.64	1. We must admit that it is quite hard to do this in the right way. At the same time, it is much less hard for some people than for others.
0.65	Some people are especially fitted for it, whether by nature, by practice, or by grace.
0.66	They are not held back by wrong shame, or by that heavy burden, the fear of people. They are both ready to take on this work of love and skilful at doing it.
0.67	So for them it is little or no cross to bear. In fact, they rather enjoy it. They find satisfaction in it, beyond the satisfaction of knowing they have done their duty.
0.68	But whether it is a big cross or a small one for us, we know that we are called to this.
0.69	And no matter how hard it is for us, we know whom we have trusted. We know he will surely keep his word: “As your day is, so your strength will be.”
0.70	2. So how should we correct our brother, so that our correction will do the most good?
0.71	First of all, let us make sure that whatever we do is done in “the spirit of love.” That means tender goodwill toward our neighbour. We should treat him as a son of our common Father, and as someone Christ died for, so that he might share in salvation.
0.72	Then, by the grace of God, love will produce love.
0.73	The warm feeling of the speaker will spread to the heart of the listener. And in time you will find that your work has not been in vain in the Lord.
0.74	3. Meanwhile, take the greatest care to speak in a spirit of humility.
0.75	Be careful not to think of yourself more highly than you ought to think.
0.76	If you think too highly of yourself, you will hardly be able to avoid looking down on your brother.
0.77	And if you show, or even feel, the smallest contempt for the people you correct, it will ruin your whole work. It will make you lose all your effort.
0.78	To prevent even the appearance of pride, you will often need to say this openly. Make it clear that you do not put yourself above him. And at the very moment you correct what is wrong, acknowledge what is good in him and thank God for it.
0.79	4. Third, take great care to speak in a spirit of gentleness as well as humility.
0.80	The apostle assures us that “the anger of men does not bring about the righteousness of God.”
0.81	Anger produces anger, even when it is dressed up with the name of zeal. It does not produce love or holiness.
0.82	So we should avoid even the appearance of anger, with all possible care.
0.83	Let there be no trace of it in your eyes, your gestures, or your tone of voice. Instead, let all of these together show a loving, humble, and calm spirit.
0.84	5. But all the while, make sure you do not trust in yourself.
0.85	Do not rely on your own wisdom, or skill, or any other ability.
0.86	For the success of everything you say or do, do not trust in yourself. Trust in the great Author of every good and perfect gift.
0.87	So while you are speaking, keep lifting up your heart to him who works all in all.
0.88	Whatever is said in the spirit of prayer will not fall to the ground.
0.89	6. That is enough about the spirit in which you should speak when you correct your neighbour.
0.90	Now I will move on to the outward way of doing it.
0.91	People have often found that beginning a correction with an honest statement of goodwill makes the words sink deep into the heart.
0.92	This will usually work far better than that great fashionable tool—flattery. Worldly people have often done surprising things by means of flattery.
0.93	But the very same things, and far greater ones, have been done much more often by a plain and simple declaration of unselfish love.
0.94	When you feel that God has lit this flame in your heart, do not hide it. Let it out fully!
0.95	It will strike like lightning.
0.96	Stubborn and hard-hearted people will melt before you. They will know that God is truly with you.
0.97	7. It is certain that the main thing in correcting is to do it with the right spirit. Still, we must admit that several small details about the outward manner are useful, so they should not be looked down on.
0.98	One of these is this: whenever you correct someone, do it very seriously. You really are in earnest, so you should look like it too.
0.99	A joking correction makes little impression, and it is soon forgotten. Besides that, it is often taken badly, as if you were making fun of the person you are correcting.
0.100	And people who are not used to making jokes do not like being joked about.
0.101	One way to give a serious tone to what you say is to use the very words of Scripture as often as you can.
0.102	We often find that God’s word has a special power, even in a private conversation. When the sinner least expects it, he feels it is “sharper than a two-edged sword.”
0.103	8. Still, there are some exceptions to this general rule of correcting seriously.
0.104	There are some special cases where, as a wise observer of human nature says,
0.105	Ridiculum acri fortius—
0.106	a little well-placed teasing will pierce deeper than solid argument.
0.107	But this applies mainly when we are dealing with people who are strangers to religion.
0.108	Sometimes we may stoop to correct such a person in a light-hearted way. When we do, it seems that Solomon’s advice gives us the right to do so: “Answer a fool according to his folly, or he will be wise in his own eyes.”
0.109	9. In other ways too, the manner of correcting can change to fit the occasion.
0.110	Sometimes it may be right to use many words and explain your view fully.
0.111	At other times it may be wiser to use few words, maybe just one sentence. At still other times it may be best to use no words at all—just a gesture, a sigh, or a look. This is especially true when the person you want to correct is far above you in rank.
0.112	Often this silent kind of correction will come with the power of God. So it will have a far better effect than a long, laboured speech.
0.113	10. One more thing: remember what Solomon said: “A word spoken at the right time—how good it is!”
0.114	It is true that if God in his providence calls you to correct someone you will probably never see again, you must take the chance right then. You must speak “in season” or “out of season.” But with people you often have the chance to see, you may wait for a good moment.
0.115	Here the poet’s advice applies.
0.116	You may speak
0.117	Si validus, si laetus erit, si denique poscet:
0.118	when he is in a good mood, or when he asks you to.
0.119	Here you may catch the
0.120	Mollia tempora fandi,—
0.121	the time when his mind is soft and gentle. Then God will both teach you how to speak and bless what is said.
0.122	11. But here let me warn you against one mistake.
0.123	People treat this as a rule that cannot be questioned: “Never try to correct a man when he is drunk.”
0.124	They say that correction is wasted then, and can do no good.
0.125	I dare not say that.
0.126	I have seen quite a few clear examples of the opposite.
0.127	Here is one. Many years ago I was passing a man in Moorfields. He was so drunk he could hardly stand. I put a paper into his hand.
0.128	He looked at it and said, “A Word—A Word to a Drunkard—that is me—Sir, Sir!
0.129	I am wrong—I know I am wrong—please let me talk with you a little.”
0.130	He held my hand for a full half-hour. And I believe he never got drunk again.
0.131	12. I beg you, brothers, by the mercies of God, do not look down on poor drunkards!
0.132	Have compassion on them!
0.133	Keep at it with them, whether the time seems right or not!
0.134	Do not let shame or the fear of people stop you from pulling these burning sticks out of the fire. Many of them already condemn themselves:
0.135	They do see the evil plight / That they are in;
0.136	but they despair. They have no hope of escaping from it. And they sink deeper and deeper into it, because no one else has any hope for them!
0.137	“I have often known sinners of every other kind converted to God,” said a respected old clergyman.
0.138	“But I have never known a habitual drunkard to be converted.”
0.139	But I have known five hundred, perhaps five thousand.
0.140	Listen! Are you one who is reading these words?
0.141	Then hear the words of the Lord!
0.142	I have a message from God for you, sinner!
0.143	This is what the Lord says: Do not throw away your hope.
0.144	I have not forgotten you.
0.145	Whoever tells you there is no help is a liar from the beginning.
0.146	Look up! See the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!
0.147	Today salvation has come to your soul. Only make sure you do not reject the one who is speaking!
0.148	Right now he is saying to you: “Son, take heart!
0.149	Your sins are forgiven!”
0.150	13. Finally: you who work hard at this labour of love, do not be discouraged, even if you see no fruit right now after doing your best.
0.151	You need patience. Then, “after you have done the will of God” in this, the harvest will come.
0.152	Never grow “weary of doing good; at the right time you will reap, if you do not lose heart.”
0.153	Follow the example of Abraham, who “against hope, still believed in hope.”
0.154	“Throw your bread on the waters, for you will find it after many days.”
0.155	14. Now I have just a few words to add for you, my brothers, who are commonly called “Methodists.”
0.156	I have never heard or read of any large revival of religion that did not come with a spirit of correcting sin.
0.157	I believe it cannot be any other way. For what is faith, unless it works by love?
0.158	That is how it was in every part of England when the present revival of religion began about fifty years ago. All who were touched by that revival—all the so-called Methodists everywhere—corrected outward sin.
0.159	And indeed, so do all who, “being justified by faith, have peace with God through Jesus Christ.”
0.160	That is how they are at the start. And if they use that precious gift, it will never be taken away.
0.161	Come, brothers, in the name of God, let us begin again!
0.162	Rich or poor, let us all rise up as one man. And let every man be sure to “rebuke his neighbour, and not let sin remain on him!”
0.163	Then all Great Britain and Ireland will know that we do not “go to war at our own cost.” Yes, “God will bless us, and all the ends of the world will fear him.”

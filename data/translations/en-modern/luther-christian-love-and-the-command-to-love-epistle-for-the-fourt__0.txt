0.1	Text: Romans 13:8-10.
0.2	8 Owe nothing to anyone, except to love one another; for he who loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law.
0.3	9 For this, You shall not commit adultery, You shall not kill, You shall not steal, You shall not covet, and if there is any other commandment, it is summed up in this word, namely, You shall love your neighbor as yourself.
0.4	10 Love works no ill to its neighbor; love, therefore, is the fulfillment of the law.
0.5	1. This lesson, like the two preceding epistle lessons, is one of admonition, and directs our attention to the fruits of faith.
0.6	Here, however, Paul briefly sums up all the fruits of faith in love.
0.7	In the preceding verses he enjoined submission to temporal government—the rendering of tribute, custom, fear and honor wherever due—since all governmental power is ordained by God.
0.8	Then follows our lesson: “Owe nothing to anyone,” and so on.
0.9	2. I shall pass over the various explanations usually invented for this command, “Owe nothing to anyone, except to love one another.”
0.10	To me it means, clearly and simply: not as men, but as Christians, are we under obligation.
0.11	Our indebtedness should be the free obligation of love.
0.12	It should not be compulsory and prescribed by law.
0.13	Paul holds up two forms of obligation: one is inspired by law, the other by love.
0.14	Legal obligations make us debtors to men; an example is when one person has a claim against another for a debt.
0.15	The duties and tribute, the obedience and honor, that we owe to political government are of this legal kind.
0.16	Though in themselves these things are not essential to the Christian—they do not justify him or make him more righteous—yet, because he must live here on earth, he is under obligation, so far as outward conduct is concerned, to place himself on a level with other men in these things, and in general to help maintain temporal order and peace.
0.17	Christ paid the tribute money as a debt (Matthew 17:27), even though he had told Peter that he was under no obligation to do so and would have committed no sin before God by leaving it undone.
0.18	3. Another obligation is love, when a Christian voluntarily makes himself a servant of all men.
0.19	Paul says (1 Corinthians 9:19), “For though I was free from all men, I brought myself into bondage to all.”
0.20	This is not a requirement of human laws; no one who fails in this duty is censured or punished for neglect of legal obligations.
0.21	The world knows nothing of the commandment to love—of the obligation to submit to and serve a fellow man.
0.22	This fact is very evident.
0.23	Let a man be wealthy, and so long as he refrains from dishonoring his neighbor’s wife, from taking his neighbor’s goods, from staining his honor or injuring his person, he is righteous in the eyes of the law.
0.24	No law punishes him for greed and stinginess; for refusing to lend, to give, to aid, and to help his wronged neighbor obtain justice.
0.25	Laws made to restrain the outward man are directed only against evil works, which they forbid and punish.
0.26	Good works are left to voluntary performance.
0.27	Civil law does not extort them by threats and punishment, but commends and rewards them, as the Law of Moses also does.
0.28	4. Paul would teach Christians so to conduct themselves toward men and civil authority as to give no occasion for complaint or censure on account of any unpaid debt to temporal law.
0.29	He would not have them fail to satisfy the claims of legal obligation, but rather go beyond its requirements, making themselves debtors voluntarily and serving those who have no claim on them.
0.30	On this subject Paul says (Romans 1:14), “I am a debtor both to Greeks and to Barbarians.”
0.31	Love’s obligation enables a man to do more than is actually required of him.
0.32	Hence the Christian always willingly renders to the state and to the individual every service demanded by temporal regulations, allowing no claims against himself in this respect.
0.33	5. Paul’s injunction, then, might be expressed thus: Owe all men, that you may owe none; owe everything, that you may owe nothing.
0.34	This sounds paradoxical. But the one indebtedness is that of love, an obligation to God.
0.35	The other is indebtedness to temporal law, an obligation in the eyes of the world.
0.36	He who makes himself a servant, who takes upon himself love’s obligation to all men, goes so far that no one dares complain of any omission; indeed, he goes farther than anyone could desire.
0.37	Thus he is made free.
0.38	He lives under obligation to no one by the very fact that he puts himself under obligation to all.
0.39	This manner of presenting the thought would be upheld by the Spirit in connection with other duties as well; for instance: Do no good work, that you may do only good works.
0.40	Never be pious and holy, if you would always be pious and holy.
0.41	As Paul says (Romans 12:16), “Do not be wise in your own conceits”; or (1 Corinthians 3:18), “If any man among you thinks that he is wise in this world, let him become a fool, that he may become wise.”
0.42	It is in this sense that we say: Owe all men, that you may owe no man; or, “Owe nothing to anyone, except to love one another.”
0.43	6. Such counsel is given with the two obligations in mind.
0.44	He who would do works truly good in the sight of God must guard against works that seem brilliant in the eyes of the world, works by which men presume to become righteous.
0.45	He who desires to be righteous and holy must guard against the holiness attained by works without faith.
0.46	Again, the seeker after wisdom must reject the wisdom of men, of nature—wisdom apart from the Spirit.
0.47	In the same way, he who would be under obligation to none must obligate himself to all in every respect.
0.48	By so doing, he retains no claim of his own.
0.49	Consequently, he soon rises above all law, for law binds only those who have claims of their own.
0.50	Rightly is it said, “Qui cedit omnibus bonis, omnibus satisfecit,” “He who surrenders all his property satisfies all men.”
0.51	How can one be under obligation when he does not, and cannot, possess anything?
0.52	It is love’s way to give all.
0.53	The best way, then, to be under obligation to none is, through love, to obligate oneself in every respect to all men.
0.54	In this sense it may be said: If you would live, die; if you would not be imprisoned, imprison yourself; if you do not wish to go to hell, descend there; if you object to being a sinner, be a sinner; if you would escape the cross, take it upon yourself; if you would conquer the devil, let him vanquish you; would you overcome a wicked person, allow him to overcome you.
0.55	The meaning of it all is that we should readily submit to God, to the devil and to men, and willingly allow them their pleasure; we are to insist on nothing, but accept all things as they come.
0.56	This is why Paul speaks as he does, “Owe nothing to anyone,” and so on, instead of letting the matter rest with the preceding injunction in verse 5, “Pay therefore to all what is due them,” and so on.
H:0.9	Love Fulfills the Law.
0.57	“For he who loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law.”
0.58	7. Since we have often spoken of the character and fruits of love, it is unnecessary to take up the subject here.
0.59	The topic is sufficiently treated in the epistle lesson for the Sunday before Lent.
0.60	We will look at the command to love as it stands in the Law of God.
0.61	Innumerable, endless, are the books and teachings produced for the direction of man’s conduct.
0.62	And still there is no limit to the making of books and laws.
0.63	Note the ecclesiastical and civil regulations, the spiritual orders and stations.
0.64	These laws and teachings might be tolerated, might be received with more favor, if they were founded upon and administered according to the one great law—the one rule or measure—of love; as the Scriptures do, which present many different laws, but all born of love, and comprehended in and subject to it.
0.65	And these laws must give way, must become invalid, when they conflict with love.
0.66	Of Love’s higher authority we find many illustrations in the Scriptures.
0.67	Christ makes particular mention of the matter in Matthew 12:3-4, where David and his companions ate the holy showbread.
0.68	Though a certain law forbade anyone but the priests to eat this holy food, Love was empress here, and free.
0.69	Love was over the Law, making it subject to herself.
0.70	The Law had to give way for the time being, had to become invalid, when David suffered hunger.
0.71	The Law had to submit to the sentence: “David is hungry and must be relieved, for Love commands, Do good to your needy neighbor.
0.72	Yield, therefore, you Law.
0.73	Do not prevent the accomplishment of this good.
0.74	Rather, accomplish it yourself.
0.75	Serve him in his time of need.
0.76	Do not interpose your prohibitions.”
0.77	In connection with this same incident, Christ teaches that we are to do good to our neighbor on the Sabbath, and to minister as necessity demands, whatever the Sabbath restrictions of the Law.
0.78	For when a brother’s need calls, Love is authority and the Law of the Sabbath is void.
0.79	8. If laws were conceived and administered in love, the number of laws would matter little.
0.80	Though one might not hear or learn all of them, he would learn from the one or two he knew the principle of love taught in all.
0.81	And though he were to know all laws, he might not discover the principle of love any more readily than he would in one.
0.82	Paul teaches this method of understanding and mastering law when he says: “Owe nothing to anyone, except to love one another”; “He who loves another has fulfilled the law”; “If there is any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, You shall love your neighbor as yourself”; “Love works no ill to its neighbor”; “Love is the fulfilling of the law.”
0.83	Every word in this epistle lesson proves Love the mistress of all law.
0.84	9. Further, no greater calamity, wrong and misery is possible on earth than the teaching and enforcing of laws without love.
0.85	In such a case, laws are nothing but a ruinous curse, proving true the proverbs, “summum jus, summa injustitia,” “The strictest right is the greatest wrong”; and again, Solomon’s words (Ecclesiastes 7:17), “Noli nimium esse justus,” “Do not be righteous to excess.”
0.86	Here is where we leave unnoticed the beam in our own eye and proceed to remove the speck from our neighbor’s eye.
0.87	Laws without love make the conscience timid and fill it with unreasonable terror and despair, to the great injury of body and soul.
0.88	Thus much trouble and labor are spent, all to no purpose.
0.89	10. An illustration in point is the incident of David in his hunger, mentioned before.
0.90	1 Samuel 21:6.
0.91	Had the priest been disposed to refuse David the holy bread, had he blindly insisted on honoring the prohibitions of the Law and failed to perceive the authority of Love, had he denied this food to him who was hungry, what would have been the result?
0.92	As far as the priest’s help went, David would have had to perish of hunger, and the priest would have been guilty of murder for the sake of the Law.
0.93	Here, indeed, “summum jus, summa injustitia”—the strictest right would have been the greatest wrong.
0.94	Moreover, on examining the heart of a priest who would be so foolish, you would find there the extreme abomination of making sin where there is no sin, and a matter of conscience where there is no occasion for it.
0.95	For he holds it a sin to eat the bread, when in reality it is an act of love and righteousness.
0.96	Then, too, he regards his act of murder—letting David die of hunger—not as a sin, but as a good work and a service to God.
0.97	11. But who can fully portray this blind, perverted, abominable folly?
0.98	It is the committing of an evil that the devil himself cannot outdo.
0.99	For it makes sin where there is no sin, and a matter of conscience without cause.
0.100	It robs men of grace, salvation, virtue, and God with all his blessings, and that without reason, falsely and deceitfully.
0.101	It emphatically denies and condemns God.
0.102	Again, it makes murder and injustice a good work, a divine service.
0.103	It puts the devil with his falsehoods in the place of God.
0.104	It establishes the worst form of idolatry and ruins body and soul, destroying the former by hunger and the latter by a terrified conscience.
0.105	It makes God into the devil, and the devil into God.
0.106	It makes hell of heaven and heaven of hell; righteousness of sin, and sin of righteousness.
0.107	This I call perversion—where the strictest justice is the greatest wrong.
0.108	To this depravity Ezekiel refers (13:18-19): “Thus says the Lord Jehovah: Woe to the women who sew pillows upon all elbows, and make kerchiefs for the heads of persons of every stature to hunt souls!
0.109	Will you hunt down the souls of my people, and preserve souls alive for yourselves?
0.110	And you have profaned me among my people for handfuls of barley and for pieces of bread, to slay the souls that should not die, and to preserve alive the souls that should not live, through your lies told to my people, who give ear to lies.”
0.111	What does this mean but that the blind teachers of the Law terrify the conscience, and put sin and death in the place of grace and life, and grace and life where there is only sin and death; and all for a handful of barley and a bit of bread?
0.112	In other words, such teachers devote themselves to laws concerning strictly external matters, things that perish with the using, such as a drink of water and a morsel of bread, wholly neglecting love and harassing the conscience with fear of sin unto eternal death; as Ezekiel goes on to say (verses 22-23): “Because with lies you have grieved the heart of the righteous, whom I have not made sad, and strengthened the hands of the wicked, so that he should not turn back from his wicked way and be saved alive; therefore you shall no more see false visions, nor practice divinations; and I will deliver my people out of your hand; and you shall know that I am Jehovah.”
0.113	12. Mark this: it is making the hearts of the righteous sad to load them with sins when their works are good; it is strengthening the hands of the wicked to make their works good when they are nothing but sin.
0.114	On this subject we read (Psalm 14:5): “There they were in great fear; for God is among the generation of the righteous.”
0.115	That is, the sting of conscience fills them with fear where there is no reason either for fear or for a troubled conscience.
0.116	What is really noble service to God is feared as sin.
0.117	The thought of the last passage is: when they ought to call upon God and serve him, they fear such conduct is sin and not divine service; again, when they have reason to fear a service that is not divine, they are secure and unafraid.
0.118	Isaiah’s words (29:13) are to the same effect: “Their fear of me is a commandment of men which has been taught them.”
0.119	The perverted people spoken of always corrupt everything.
0.120	They confidently call on God where there is only the devil; they fearfully refrain from calling on God where God is.
0.121	13. Such, note well, is the wretched condition of those who are blindly occupied with laws and works and fail to grasp the purpose of law and its mistress, Love.
0.122	Note, also, in the case of our miserable churchmen and their followers, how rigidly they cling to their own inventions!
0.123	Though all the world come to ruin, their devices must be upheld; they must be perpetuated regardless of bodily sickness and death, or of suffering and ruin for the soul.
0.124	They even regard such destruction and ruin as divine service, and know no fear or remorse of conscience.
0.125	Indeed, so firmly entrenched are they in their wickedness that they will never turn back from it.
0.126	Moreover, should one of their wretched number be permitted to relieve the distress of his body and soul—to eat meat, to marry—he is afraid, he feels remorse of conscience; he is uncertain about sin and law, about death and hell; he does not call on God, nor serve him; all this, even though the body should die ten deaths and the soul go to the devil a hundred times.
0.127	14. Observe, then, the state of the world; how little flesh and blood can accomplish even in their best efforts; how dangerous it is to undertake to rule by law alone—indeed, how impossible it is, without great danger, to govern and instruct souls with mere laws, ignoring love and the Spirit, in whose hands lies the full power of all law.
0.128	It is written (Deuteronomy 33:2), “At his right hand was a fiery law for them.”
0.129	This is the law of love in the Spirit.
0.130	It shall govern all the laws at the left hand; that is, the external laws of the world.
0.131	It is said (Exodus 28:30) that the priest must wear upon his breast, in the breastplate, “the Urim and the Thummim”; that is, Light and Perfection, signifying the priest’s office to illuminate the Law—to give its true sense—and to keep and teach it faultlessly.
0.132	15. In the framing, the establishment and the observance of all laws, the aim should be, not the furtherance of the laws in themselves, not the advancement of works, but the exercise of love.
0.133	That is the true purpose of law, according to Paul here: “He who loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law.”
0.134	Therefore, when the law contributes to the injury rather than the benefit of our neighbor, it should be disregarded.
0.135	The same law may at one time benefit our neighbor and at another time injure him.
0.136	Consequently, it should be regulated according to its benefit to him.
0.137	Law should be made to serve in the same way that food and clothing and the other necessities of life serve.
0.138	We consider not the food and clothing in themselves, but their benefit to our needy neighbor.
0.139	And we stop dispensing them as soon as we see that they no longer add to his comfort.
0.140	16. Suppose you were to come across a man foolish enough to act with no other thought than that food and clothing are truly good things, and so proceed to stuff a needy person with unlimited food and drink until he chokes, and to clothe him until he suffocates, and then not to stop.
0.141	Suppose that to the command, “Stop, you have suffocated him, you have already overfed and overclothed him, and it is all wasted effort now,” the foolish man should reply: “You heretic, would you forbid good works?
0.142	Food, drink and clothing are good things; therefore we must not stop dispensing them; we cannot do too much.”
0.143	And suppose he went on forcing food and clothing on the man.
0.144	Tell me, what would you think of such a man?
0.145	He is a fool beyond all foolishness; he is madder than madness itself.
0.146	But such is very nearly the character of our churchmen today, and of those who are so blind in the exercise of law as to act as if works were the only requirement, and to suffocate body and soul, being ignorant that the one purpose of law is to call forth the exercise of love.
0.147	They make works superior to love, and a maid superior to her mistress.
0.148	Such perversion prevails to an extent distressing to think of, not to mention hearing and seeing it, or, more than that, practicing and permitting it ourselves.
0.149	17. The commandment of love is not a long one; it is short.
0.150	It is one injunction, not many.
0.151	It is not even a commandment, and at the same time it is all commandments.
0.152	Brief, and a unit in itself, its meaning is easily grasped.
0.153	But in its exercise it is far-reaching, for it includes and regulates all commandments.
0.154	So far as works are enjoined, it is no commandment at all; it names no particular work.
0.155	Yet it represents all commandments, because properly the fulfillment of all commandments is the fulfillment of this one.
0.156	The commandment of love suspends every commandment, yet it perpetuates all.
0.157	Its whole purpose is that we may recognize no commandment, no work, except as love dictates.
0.158	18. Since life on earth apart from works is an impossibility, there must necessarily be various commandments involving works.
0.159	Yet Love is supreme over these requirements, dictating the omission or the performance of works according to its own best interests, and permitting no works opposed to itself.
0.160	To illustrate: a driver, holding the reins, guides team and wagon at will.
0.161	If he were content merely to hold the reins, regardless of whether or not the team followed the road, the entire outfit—team, wagon, reins and driver—would soon be wrecked; the driver would be lying drowned in a ditch or a pool, or would have his neck broken going over stumps and rocks.
0.162	But if he skillfully regulates the movement of the outfit according to the road, observing where it is safe and where unsafe, he will proceed securely because wisely.
0.163	Were he, in his self-conceit, to drive straight ahead, trying to make the road conform to the movement of the wagon at his pleasure, he would soon see how beautifully his plan would work.
0.164	19. So it is when men are governed by laws and works, and the laws are not regulated according to the people.
0.165	The case is that of the driver who would regulate the road by the movements of the wagon.
0.166	True, the road is often well suited to the straight course of the wagon.
0.167	But just as truly the road is, in certain places, crooked and uneven, and then the wagon must conform to the course and condition of the road.
0.168	Men must adapt themselves to laws and regulations wherever possible and where the laws are beneficial.
0.169	But where laws prove harmful to men’s interests, the laws must yield.
0.170	The ruler must wisely make allowance for love, suspending works and laws.
0.171	Hence philosophers say that prudence—or circumspection or discretion, as the churchmen put it—is the guide and regulator of all virtues.
0.172	20. We read in a book of the ancient fathers that on a certain occasion when they had assembled, the question was raised: which is really the noblest work?
0.173	Various answers were given.
0.174	One said prayer, another fasting; but St. Anthony was of the opinion that of all works and virtues, discretion is the best and surest way to heaven.
0.175	These, however, were but childish, unspiritual ideas concerning their own self-chosen works.
0.176	A Christian views the matter in quite a different light, and more judiciously.
0.177	He concludes that neither discretion nor rashness avails before God.
0.178	Only faith and love are of service with him.
0.179	But love is true discretion; love is the driver and the true discretion in righteous works.
0.180	It always looks to the good of the neighbor, to the betterment of his condition; just as the discretion of the world looks to the general welfare of the governed in the adjustment of political laws.
0.181	Let this suffice on this point.
0.182	21. But the question arises: How can love fulfill the Law when love is only one of the fruits of faith, and we have often said that only faith in Christ removes our sins, justifies us and satisfies all the demands of the Law?
0.183	How can we make the two claims agree?
0.184	Christ says, too (Matthew 7:12): “All things, therefore, whatever you wish that men would do to you, even so do also to them: for this is the law and the prophets.”
0.185	Thus he shows that love for one’s neighbor fulfills both the Law and the prophets.
0.186	Again, he says (Matthew 22:37-40): “You shall love the Lord your God ... your neighbor as yourself.
0.187	On these two the whole law hangs, and the prophets.”
0.188	Where, then, does Paul stand, who says (Romans 3:31): “Do we then render the law void through faith?
0.189	By no means: rather, we establish the law.”
0.190	Again (Romans 3:28): “We hold, therefore, that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law.”
0.191	And again (Romans 1:17), “The righteous shall live by faith.”
0.192	22. I reply: As we have often said, we must properly distinguish between faith and love.
0.193	Faith deals with the heart, and love with works.
0.194	Faith removes our sins, makes us acceptable, justifies us.
0.195	And once we are accepted and justified as to our person, love is given us in the Holy Spirit and we delight in doing good.
0.196	Now, it is the nature of the Law to attack our person and demand good works; and it will not cease to demand until it gains its point.
0.197	We cannot do good works apart from the Spirit and love.
0.198	The Law compels us to know ourselves with our imperfections, and to recognize that we must become altogether different people if we are to satisfy the Law.
0.199	The Law does not demand so much of the heart as of works; in fact, it demands nothing but works and ignores the heart.
0.200	It leaves the individual to discover, from the works required, that he must become an altogether different person.
0.201	But faith, when it comes, creates a nature capable of accomplishing the works the Law demands.
0.202	Thus the Law is fulfilled.
0.203	So Paul’s sayings on the subject are beautiful and fitting.
0.204	The Law demands works of us; it must be fulfilled by works.
0.205	Hence it cannot in every sense be said that faith fulfills the Law.
0.206	However, it prepares the way and enables us to fulfill it, for the Law demands not us, but our works.
0.207	The Law constrains us—teaches us that we must be changed before we can accomplish its works; it makes us aware of our inability as we are.
0.208	On the other hand, love and works do not change us and do not justify us.
0.209	We must be changed in person and justified before we can love and do good works.
0.210	Our love and our works are evidence of justification and of a change, since they are impossible until the individual is free from sin and made righteous.
0.211	23. This explanation is given to enable us to perceive the true nature of the Law, of faith and of love; to assign to each its own mission; and rightly to understand the declarations of Scripture in their harmonious relations: that while faith justifies, it does not fulfill the Law, and that while love does not justify, it does fulfill the Law.
0.212	The Law requires love and works, but does not mention the heart.
0.213	The heart is conscious of the Law, but love is not.
0.214	Just as the Law, in requiring works before faith exists, is a sign to the individual that leads him to recognize his utter lack of faith and righteousness and to conclude that he is conquered, so love, in its fulfillment of the Law after faith has come, is a sign and a proof to the individual of his faith and righteousness.
0.215	Law and love, then, bear witness to him concerning his unrighteousness or his righteousness.
0.216	After faith comes, love is evidence of righteousness.
0.217	Before faith, man feels the Law’s oppression because he knows he does not possess what the Law requires.
0.218	And the Law does not require a changed heart, but works.
0.219	Love and works do not bring about the fulfillment of the Law; they are themselves its fulfillment.
0.220	24. Now, though faith does not fulfill the Law, it contains that which brings about its fulfillment; it secures the Spirit and love by which the end is accomplished.
0.221	On the other hand, though love does not justify us, it makes manifest the faith by which we are justified.
0.222	Briefly, as Paul says here, “Love is the fulfillment of the law.”
0.223	His thought is: fulfillment of the Law is one thing, and effecting or supplying its fulfillment is another.
0.224	Love fulfills the Law in the sense that love itself is its fulfillment; but faith fulfills it in the sense that it supplies that by which it is fulfilled.
0.225	For faith loves and works, as is said in Galatians 5:6, “Faith works through love.”
0.226	The water fills the pitcher; so does the cupbearer.
0.227	The water fills of itself; the cupbearer fills with the water—“effective et formaliter implere,” as the sophists would say.
0.228	25. Faith is ever the doer, and love the deed.
0.229	The law requires the deed and thus forces the doer to be changed.
0.230	The Law is then fulfilled by the deed, which, however, the doer must perform.
0.231	Thus Paul rejects the fancies of the sophists, who in the matter of love would distinguish between the external work and the inner affection, saying: “Love is an inner affection that loves our neighbor when in our heart we wish him well.”
0.232	Its expression in works, however, they call the fruit of love.
0.233	But we will not discuss this idea.
0.234	Note that Paul calls love not only an affection, but an affectionate good deed.
0.235	Faith and the heart are the doer and fulfiller of the Law.
0.236	Paul says, “He who loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law.”
0.237	And love is the deed, the fulfilling; for he says, “Love is the fulfillment of the law.”
0.238	26. Another question arises: How can love for our neighbor be the fulfillment of the Law when we are required to love God supremely, even above our neighbor?
0.239	I reply: Christ answers the question when he tells us (Matthew 22:39) that the second commandment is like the first.
0.240	He makes love to God and love to our neighbor the same love.
0.241	The reason for this is, first: God, having no need of our works and benefactions for himself, bids us do for our neighbor what we would do for God.
0.242	For himself he asks only our faith and our acknowledgment of him as God.
0.243	The purpose of proclaiming his honor and rendering him praise and thanks here on earth is that our neighbor may be converted and brought into fellowship with God.
0.244	Such service is called the love of God, and is performed out of love to God; but it is exercised for the benefit of our neighbor only.
0.245	27. The second reason why God makes love to our neighbor an obligation equal to love to himself is this: God has made worldly wisdom foolish, desiring henceforth to be loved amid crosses and afflictions.
0.246	Paul says (1 Corinthians 1:21), “Seeing that in the wisdom of God the world through its wisdom did not come to know God, it was God’s good pleasure through the foolishness of the preaching to save those who believe.”
0.247	Therefore upon the cross he submitted himself to death and misery, and laid the same submission upon all his disciples.
0.248	Those who refused to love him before, when he bestowed on them food and drink, blessing and honor, must now love him in hunger and sorrow, in adversity and disgrace.
0.249	All works of love, then, must be directed to our wretched, needy neighbors.
0.250	In these lowly ones we are to find and love God, in them we are to serve and honor him, and only in this way can we do it.
0.251	The commandment to love God is wholly merged in that to love our neighbors.
0.252	28. These facts restrain those elusive, soaring spirits who seek after God only in great and glorious undertakings.
0.253	It stops the mouths of those who strive after greatness like his, who would force their way into heaven, presuming to serve and love him with their brilliant works.
0.254	But they miss him by passing him by in their earthly neighbor, in whom God would be loved and honored.
0.255	Therefore they will hear, on the last day, the sentence (Matthew 25:42), “I was hungry, and you did not give me to eat,” and so on.
0.256	For Christ laid aside his divinity and took upon himself the form of a servant for the very purpose of bringing down and centering upon our neighbor the love we extend to himself.
0.257	Yet we leave the Lord lying here in his humiliation while we gaze open-mouthed into heaven and make great pretensions of love and service to God.
H:0.37	All Commandments Summed Up in Love.
0.258	“For this, You shall not commit adultery, You shall not kill, You shall not steal, You shall not covet; and if there is any other commandment, it is briefly summed up in this word, namely, You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”
0.259	29. Since love is the chief element of all law, it comprehends, as has been made sufficiently clear, all commandments.
0.260	Its one concern is to be useful to man and not harmful; therefore it readily finds the way.
0.261	Recognizing that man, out of his ardent self-love, seeks to promote his own interests and avoid injuring them, love endeavors to take the same course toward others.
0.262	We will consider the commandment just cited, noticing how ingeniously and wisely it is arranged.
0.263	It brings out four thoughts.
0.264	First, it states who is under obligation to love: you—the nearest, noblest, best individual we can command.
0.265	No one can fulfill the Law of God for another; each must do it for himself.
0.266	As Paul says (Galatians 6:5), “Each man shall bear his own burden.”
0.267	And (2 Corinthians 5:10): “For we must all be laid open before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive back the things done in the body, according to what he has done, be it good or bad.”
0.268	So it is said, “You, you yourself, must love”; not, “Let someone else love for you.”
0.269	Though one can and should pray that God may be gracious to another and help him, yet no one will be saved unless he himself fulfills God’s command.
0.270	It is not enough merely to pray that another may escape punishment, as the sellers of indulgences teach; much rather, we should pray that he may become righteous and keep God’s precepts.
0.271	30. Second, the commandment names the most noble virtue—love.
0.272	It does not say, “You shall feed your neighbor, give him drink, clothe him,” all of which are inestimably good works; it says, “You shall love him.”
0.273	Love is the chief virtue, the fountain of all virtues.
0.274	Love gives food and drink; it clothes, comforts, persuades, relieves and rescues.
0.275	What shall we say of it? For behold, he who loves gives himself, body and soul, property and honor, all his powers inner and outward, for the benefit of his needy neighbor, whether friend or enemy; he withholds nothing by which he may serve another.
0.276	There is no virtue like love; no special work can be assigned to it, as in the case of limited virtues such as chastity, mercy, patience, meekness and the like.
0.277	Love does all things.
0.278	It will suffer in life and in death, in every condition, and that even for its enemies.
0.279	Well may Paul say here that all other commandments are briefly comprehended in the injunction, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”
0.280	31. Third, the commandment names, as the sphere of our love, the noblest field, the dearest friend—our neighbor.
0.281	It does not say, “You shall love the rich, the mighty, the learned, the saint.”
0.282	No, the unrestrained love set forth in this most perfect commandment does not divide itself among a few.
0.283	With it there is no respect of persons.
0.284	It is the nature of false, carnal, worldly love to have respect of persons, and to love only so long as it hopes to gain some profit.
0.285	When such hope ceases, that love also ceases.
0.286	The commandment of our text, however, requires of us free, spontaneous love to all men, whoever they may be, and whether friend or foe, a love that seeks no profit and ministers only what is beneficial.
0.287	Such love is most active and powerful in serving the poor, the needy, the sick, the wicked, the simple-minded and the hostile; among these it is always and under all circumstances necessary to suffer and endure, to serve and do good.
0.288	32. Note here that this commandment makes us all equal before God, without regard to the distinctions attaching to our stations in life, our persons, offices and occupations.
0.289	Since the commandment is to all—to every human being—a sovereign, if he is a human being, must confess the poorest beggar, the most wretched leper, to be his neighbor and his equal in the sight of God.
0.290	He is under obligation, according to this commandment, not to extend a measure of help, but to serve that neighbor with all he has and all he controls.
0.291	If he loves him as God here commands him to, he must give the beggar preference over his crown and all his realm; and if the beggar’s necessity requires it, he must give his life.
0.292	He is under obligation to love his neighbor, and must admit that such a person is his neighbor.
0.293	33. Is not this a superior, a noble commandment, which completely levels the most unequal individuals?
0.294	Is it not wonderfully comforting to the beggar to have servants and lovers of such honor? Is it not wonderful that his poverty commands the services of a king in his wealth? that to his sores and wounds are made subject the crown of wealth and the sweet fragrance of royal splendor?
0.295	But how strange it would seem to us to see kings and queens, princes and princesses, serving beggars and lepers, as we read that St. Elizabeth did!
0.296	Even this, however, would be a slight thing compared with what Christ has done.
0.297	No one can ever equal him in the obedience by which he has exalted this commandment.
0.298	He is a king whose honor surpasses that of all other kings; indeed, he is the Son of God.
0.299	And yet he puts himself on a level with the worst sinners, and serves them even to the point of dying for them.
0.300	Were ten kings of the earth to serve one beggar to the utmost, it would be a remarkable thing; but of what significance would it be in comparison with the service Christ has rendered?
0.301	The kings would be put to utter shame and would have to acknowledge that their service was unworthy of notice.

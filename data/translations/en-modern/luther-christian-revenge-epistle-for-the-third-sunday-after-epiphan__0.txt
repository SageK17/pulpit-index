0.9	"Do not be wise in your own opinions."
0.10	64. The lesson as it is read in the Church ends here.
0.11	We will therefore note the remaining portion only briefly.
0.12	"Your own opinions," as used here, means the stubborn attitude toward temporal things held by the person who is unwilling to be taught, who himself knows best in everything, who yields to no one, and who calls good whatever agrees with his own ideas.
0.13	The Christian should be more willing to give way in temporal affairs.
0.14	Let him not be quarrelsome, but rather yielding, since the Word of God and faith are not involved; it is only a question of personal honor, of friends, and of worldly things.
0.15	"Repay no one evil for evil."
0.16	65. In the counsel above (verse 14) to "curse not," the writer of the epistle has in mind those who are unable to avenge themselves or to return evil for evil.
0.17	These have no other option but to curse, to call down evil upon their oppressors.
0.18	Here, however, the reference is to those who have equal power to repay one another evil for evil, malice for malice, whether by things done or things left undone—and usually they are left undone.
0.19	But the Christian should return good for evil, and not leave it undone.
0.20	God lets his sun shine on the evil and on the good.
0.21	Matthew 5:45.
0.22	"Take thought for what is honorable in the sight of all men."
0.23	66. This command is similar to the one he gives the Thessalonians (1 Thess 5:22), "Abstain from every appearance of evil"; and the Philippians (ch. 4:8): "Whatever things are true, whatever things are honorable, whatever things are just, whatever things are pure, whatever things are lovely, whatever things are of good report; if there is any virtue, and if there is any praise, think on these things."
0.24	The reference is purely to our outward conduct.
0.25	Paul would not have the Christian think himself free to do as he pleases, regardless of whether others approve.
0.26	Only in the things of faith does the Christian have that privilege.
0.27	His outward conduct should be beyond reproach, acceptable to all men; in keeping with the teaching of First Corinthians 10:32-33, to please all men, giving offense neither to Jews nor to Gentiles; and obedient to Peter's advice (1 Pet 2:12), "Keep your behavior proper among the Gentiles."
0.28	"If it is possible, as far as it lies in you, be at peace with all men."
0.29	67. Outward peace among people is meant here—peace with Christians and heathen, with the godly and the wicked, the high and the low.
0.30	We must give no occasion for strife; rather, we are to endure every ill patiently, never allowing peace to be disturbed on our account.
0.31	We must not return evil for evil, blow for blow; for whoever does so gives rise to contention.
0.32	Paul adds, "As far as it lies in you."
0.33	We are to avoid injuring anyone, so that we are not the ones who cause contention.
0.34	We must show friendliness to all men, even though they are not friendly to us.
0.35	It is impossible to keep peace at all times.
0.36	The saying goes, "I can live in peace only as long as my neighbor is willing."
0.37	But it is in our power to leave others in peace, friends and foes, and to endure the contentions of all.
0.38	"Oh yes," you say, "but where would we be then?" Listen:
0.39	"Do not avenge yourselves, beloved, but give place to the wrath of God: for it is written, Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay, says the Lord."
0.40	68. Note that in forbidding us to return blow for blow and to resort to vengeance, the apostle implies that our enjoyment of peace depends on our quietly enduring the disturbance caused by others.
0.41	He not only assures us that we will be avenged, but he also frightens us away from usurping the office of God, to whom alone vengeance and retribution belong.
0.42	Indeed, he rather laments the fate of the Christian's enemies, who expose themselves to God's wrath; he would move us to pity them, seeing that we must give place to wrath and let them fall into the hands of God.
0.43	The vengeance and wrath of God are dealt out in various ways: through the instrument of the political government; at the hands of the devil; by illness, hunger, and pestilence; by fire and water; by war, hostility, and disgrace; and by every possible kind of misfortune on earth.
0.44	Every creature may serve as the rod and the weapon of God when he intends to chastise.
0.45	As it says in the Wisdom of Solomon 5:17: "He shall ... make the creature his weapon for vengeance on his enemies."
0.46	69. So Paul says, "Give place to wrath."
0.47	I have inserted the words "of God" to make the meaning of the text clearer; the wrath of God is meant, and not the wrath of man.
0.48	The thought is not of giving place to the anger of our enemies.
0.49	True, there may be occasion even for that, but Paul is not referring here to man's anger.
0.50	Evidently he means misfortunes and plagues, which are regarded as expressions of God's wrath.
0.51	Possibly the apostle left out the phrase to avoid giving the idea that only the final wrath of God is meant—his anger on the last day, when he will inflict punishment without any instrument.
0.52	Paul would include here all wrath, whether temporal or eternal, to which God gives expression in his chastisements.
0.53	This is an Old Testament way of speaking.
0.54	Phinehas says (Josh 22:18), "Tomorrow he will be angry with ...
0.55	Israel." And Moses in several places speaks of God's anger being kindled.
0.56	See Numbers 11:1, 10, 33.
0.57	I mention these things in order to teach that when the political government wields the sword of punishment against its enemies, it should be regarded as an expression of God's wrath; and that the statement in Deuteronomy 32:35, "Vengeance is mine," does not refer only to punishment inflicted by God directly, without any instrument.
0.58	"But if your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he is thirsty, give him something to drink; for by doing so you will heap coals of fire on his head."
0.59	70. This teaching confirms what I have already said—that the Christian's enemies are to be pitied because they are subject to the wrath of God.
0.60	Therefore it is not Christian to injure them; rather, we should do them favors.
0.61	Paul here introduces a quotation from Solomon.
0.62	Proverbs 25:21-22. Heaping coals of fire on the head, in my view, means doing favors for the enemy.
0.63	Set aflame by our kindness, he eventually becomes displeased with himself and more kindly disposed toward us.
0.64	Coals here are benefits, or favors.
0.65	The coals in the censer likewise stand for the favors, or blessings, of God; they are a figure of our prayers, which should rise with fervor.
0.66	Some say that the coals represent the Law and judgments of God (see Psalm 18:8, "Coals were kindled by it"), reasoning that as a result of the Christian's favors, his enemy is compelled to censure himself and to feel the weight of God's Law and his judgments.
0.67	I do not think a Christian should desire punishment to fall on his enemy, though such an explanation of the sentence is not unfitting.
0.68	In fact, it rather agrees with the command, "Give place to wrath"; that is, do good, and then wrath—the coals—will readily fall on the enemy.
0.69	"Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good."
0.70	71. With this concluding counsel, it seems to me, Paul himself explains the phrase "coals of fire" in keeping with the first idea—that the malice of an enemy is to be overcome with good.
0.71	Overcoming by force is the same as lending yourself to evil and wronging the enemy who wrongs you.
0.72	By such a course your enemy overcomes you, and you are made evil like him.
0.73	But if you overcome him with good, he will be made righteous like you.
0.74	A spiritual overcoming is meant here; the disposition, the heart, the soul—yes, the devil who stirs up the evil—are overcome.

8.153	9. And see what great works pride does.
8.154	Lay it up in your hearts how alike, how nearly on a par, as it were, are the works it does and the works of charity.
8.155	Charity feeds the hungry, and so does pride: charity, that God may be praised; pride, that itself may be praised.
8.156	Charity clothes the naked, so does pride; charity fasts, so does pride; charity buries the dead, so does pride.
8.157	All the good works which charity wishes to do, and does, pride on the other hand drives at the same, and, so to speak, keeps her horses up to the mark.
8.158	But charity stands between her and it, and leaves no room for ill-driven pride; not ill-driving, but ill-driven.
8.159	Woe to the man whose charioteer is pride, for he must go headlong!
8.160	But in the good that is done, that it may not be pride that sets us on, who knows? Who sees it? Where is it? The works we see: mercy feeds, pride also feeds; mercy takes in the stranger, pride also takes in the stranger; mercy intercedes for the poor, pride also intercedes.
8.161	How is this? In the works we see no difference.
8.162	I dare to say something, but not I; Paul has said it: charity dies, that is, a man having charity confesses the name of Christ and suffers martyrdom; pride also confesses, and also suffers martyrdom.
8.163	The one has charity, the other does not have charity.
8.164	But let him who does not have charity hear from the apostle: “If I distribute all my goods to the poor, and if I give my body to be burned, and do not have charity, it profits me nothing.”
8.165	So then the divine Scripture calls us away from the outward display of the face to that which is within; from this surface which is paraded before men, it calls us away to that which is within.
8.166	Return to your own conscience; question it.
8.167	Do not consider what blossoms outwardly, but what root there is in the ground.
8.168	Is lust rooted there?
8.169	There may be a show of good deeds; truly good works there cannot be.
8.170	Is charity rooted there?
8.171	Have no fear: nothing evil can come of that.
8.172	The proud man caresses; love is severe.
8.173	The one clothes, the other strikes.
8.174	For the one clothes in order to please men, the other strikes in order to correct by discipline.
8.175	More acceptable is the blow of charity than the alms of pride.
8.176	Come within, then, brethren; and in all things, whatever you do, look to God your witness.
8.177	See whether He sees with what mind you do it.
8.178	If your heart does not accuse you of doing it for the sake of display, it is well: do not fear.
8.179	But when you do good, do not fear that another may see you.
8.180	Fear lest you do it to the end that you may be praised; let the other see it, that God may be praised.
8.181	For if you hide it from the eyes of man, you hide it from the imitation of man; you withdraw from God His praise.
8.182	There are two to whom you give the alms; two hunger, one for bread, the other for righteousness.
8.183	Between these two famishing souls (as it is written, “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be filled”), between these two famishing persons you, the doer of the good work, are set; if charity does the work on occasion of the one, in it she has pity on both; she would help both.
8.184	For the one craves what he may eat, the other craves what he may imitate.
8.185	You feed the one; give yourself as a pattern to the other; so you have given alms to both: the one you have caused to thank you for killing his hunger, the other you have made to imitate you by setting him an example.
8.186	10. Show mercy, then, as men of merciful hearts; because in loving enemies also, you love brethren.
8.187	Do not think that John has given no precept concerning love of our enemy because he has not ceased to speak of brotherly love.
8.188	You love brethren. “How,” you say, “do we love brethren?”
8.189	I ask why you love an enemy.
8.190	Why do you love him?
8.191	That he may be healthy in this life? What if it is not expedient for him?
8.192	That he may be rich? What if he is blinded by his very riches?
8.193	That he may marry a wife? What if he has a bitter life of it?
8.194	That he may have children? What if they turn out bad?
8.195	Uncertain, therefore, are these things which you seem to wish for your enemy in loving him; they are uncertain.
8.196	Wish for him that he may have eternal life with you; wish for him that he may be your brother: then, when you love him, you love a brother.
8.197	For you love in him not what he is, but what you wish him to be.
8.198	I once said to you, my beloved, if I am not mistaken: There is a log of timber lying in sight; a good workman has seen the log, not yet planed, just as it was hewn from the forest; he has taken a liking to it; he would make something out of it.
8.199	For indeed he did not love it so that it should always remain as it is.
8.200	In his art he has seen what it shall be, not in his liking what it is; and his liking is for the thing he will make of it, not for the thing it is.
8.201	So God loved us sinners.
8.202	We say that God loved sinners, for He says, “Those who are well have no need of the Physician, but those who are sick.”
8.203	Did He love us sinners so that we should still remain sinners?
8.204	Our Carpenter saw us as timber from the wood, and had in His thoughts the building He would make of it, not the unwrought timber that it was.
8.205	So too you see your enemy striving against you, raging, biting with words, provoking with insults, harassing with hatred: you have regard to this in him, that he is a man.
8.206	You see all these things that are against you, that they were done by man; and you see in him that he was made by God.
8.207	Now, that he was made a man was God’s doing; but that he hates you is his own doing; that he bears you ill will is his own doing.
8.208	And what do you say in your mind?
8.209	“Lord, be merciful to him, forgive him his sins, strike terror into him, change him.”
8.210	You do not love in him what he is, but what you wish him to be.
8.211	Consequently, when you love an enemy, you love a brother.
8.212	Therefore, perfect love is the loving of an enemy; and this perfect love is in brotherly love.
8.213	And let no one say that John the apostle has admonished us somewhat less, and the Lord Christ somewhat more.
8.214	John has admonished us to love the brethren; Christ has admonished us to love even enemies.
8.215	Mark to what end Christ has bidden you to love your enemies.
8.216	That they may remain enemies always?
8.217	If He bade it to this end, that they should remain enemies, you hate, you do not love.
8.218	Mark how He Himself loved: because He would not have them remain the persecutors they were, He said, “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.”
8.219	Those whom He willed to be forgiven, He willed to be changed; those whom He willed to be changed, He deigned to make brethren out of enemies, and did in truth make them so.
8.220	He was killed, was buried, rose again, ascended into heaven; He sent the Holy Spirit to His disciples; they began to preach His name with boldness; they did miracles in the name of Him who was crucified and slain; those slayers of the Lord saw them; and those who in rage had shed His blood drank it by believing.
8.221	11. These things I have said, brethren, and at some length; yet because charity was to be commended to you more earnestly, beloved, in this way it had to be commended.
8.222	For if there is no charity in you, we have said nothing.
8.223	But if it is in you, we have, as it were, poured oil on the flames.
8.224	And in whom it was not, perhaps it has been kindled by words.
8.225	In one, what was there has grown; in another, what was not has begun to be.
8.226	To this end, therefore, we have said these things, that you may not be slow to love your enemies.
8.227	Does anyone rage against you? He rages, you pray; he hates, you pity.
8.228	It is the fever of his soul that hates you: he will be well, and will thank you.
8.229	How do physicians love those who are sick?
8.230	Is it the sick that they love?
8.231	If they love them as sick, they wish them to be always sick.
8.232	To this end they love the sick: not that they should remain sick, but that from being sick they should be made well.
8.233	And how much they very often have to suffer from the frenzied!
8.234	What insulting language! Very often they are even struck by them.
8.235	He attacks the fever, forgives the man.
8.236	And what shall I say, brethren? Does he love his enemy?
8.237	No, he hates his enemy, the disease; for it is this that he hates, and he loves the man by whom he is struck: he hates the fever.
8.238	For by whom or by what is he struck? By the disease, by the sickness, by the fever.
8.239	He takes away that which strives against him, that there may remain that from which he will have thanks.
8.240	So you do likewise. If your enemy hates you, and hates you unjustly, know that the lust of the world reigns in him; therefore he hates you.
8.241	If you also hate him, you in turn render evil for evil.
8.242	What does it achieve, to render evil for evil?
8.243	I wept for one sick man who hated you; now I mourn for you, if you also hate.
8.244	But he attacks your property; he takes from you I know not what things that you have on earth; therefore you hate him, because he puts you in straits on earth.
8.245	Do not be straitened; move yourself to heaven above; there you will have your heart where there is wide room, so that you may not be straitened in the hope of eternal life.
8.246	Consider what the things are that he takes from you: he would not take even these from you except by permission of Him who “scourges every son whom He receives.”
8.247	He, this same enemy of yours, is in a manner the instrument in the hands of God by which you may be healed.
8.248	If God knows it to be good for you that he should despoil you, He permits him; if He knows it to be good for you that you should receive blows, He permits him to strike you: by means of him He cares for you. Wish that he may be made well.
8.249	12. “No one has seen God at any time.”
8.250	See, beloved: “If we love one another, God will dwell in us, and His love will be perfected in us.”
8.251	Begin to love; you will be perfected.
8.252	Have you begun to love?
8.253	God has begun to dwell in you: love Him who has begun to dwell in you, that by dwelling in you more perfectly He may make you perfect.
8.254	“By this we know that we dwell in Him and He in us, because He has given us of His Spirit.”
8.255	It is well: thanks be to God!
8.256	We come to know that He dwells in us.
8.257	And how do we come to know this very thing, namely, that we do know that He dwells in us?
8.258	Because John himself has said this: “Because He has given us of His Spirit.”
8.259	How do we know that He has given us of His Spirit?
8.260	This very thing, that He has given you of His Spirit, how do you come to know it?
8.261	Ask your own inmost heart: if it is full of charity, you have the Spirit of God.
8.262	How do we know that by this you know that the Spirit of God dwells in you?
8.263	“Because the love of God is poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit who is given to us.”
8.264	13. “And we have seen and are witnesses that God has sent His Son to be the Savior of the world.”
8.265	Set your minds at rest, you who are sick: such a Physician has come, and do you despair?
8.266	Great were the diseases, incurable were the wounds, desperate was the sickness.
8.267	Do you note the greatness of your ill, and not note the omnipotence of the Physician?
8.268	You are desperate, but He is omnipotent; His witnesses are these who were first healed and who announce the Physician; yet even they are made well in hope rather than in reality.
8.269	For so says the apostle: “For we are saved by hope.”
8.270	We have begun, therefore, to be made well in faith; but our wholeness will be perfected “when this corruptible has put on incorruption, and this mortal has put on immortality.”
8.271	This is hope, not the reality.
8.272	But he who rejoices in hope will hold the reality also; whereas he who does not have the hope will not be able to attain to the reality.
8.273	14. “Whoever confesses that Jesus is the Son of God, God dwells in him, and he in God.”
8.274	Now we may say it in few words: “Whoever confesses”; not in word but in deed, not with the tongue but with the life.
8.275	For many confess in words but deny in deeds: “And we have known and believed the love that God has in us.”
8.276	And again, by what have you come to know this?
8.277	“Love is God.” He has already said it above; behold, he says it again.
8.278	Love could not be more highly commended to you than by being called God.
8.279	Perhaps you were ready to despise a gift of God.
8.280	And do you despise God?
8.281	“Love is God, and he who dwells in love dwells in God, and God dwells in him.”
8.282	Each mutually inhabits the other: He who holds, and he who is held.
8.283	You dwell in God, but in order that you may be held; God dwells in you, but in order that He may hold you, lest you fall.
8.284	Lest perhaps you imagine that you become a house of God in the same way that your house supports your flesh: if the house in which you are withdraws itself from under you, you fall; but if you withdraw yourself, God does not fall.
8.285	When you forsake Him, He is none the less; when you have returned to Him, He is none the greater.
8.286	You are healed, yet on Him you will bestow nothing; you are made clean, you are made new, you are set right: He is a medicine for the unhealthy, a rule for the crooked, light for the darkened, a dwelling for the forsaken.
8.287	All, therefore, is conferred on you: see that you do not imagine that anything is conferred on God by your coming to Him; no, not so much as a slave.
8.288	Shall God, indeed, not have servants if you are unwilling, if all are unwilling?
8.289	God does not need the servants, but the servants need God; therefore the Psalm says, “I have said to the Lord, You are my God.”
8.290	He is the true Lord.
8.291	And what does it say?
8.292	“For You have no need of my goods.”
8.293	You need the good you have from your servant.
8.294	Your servant needs the good he has from you, that you may feed him; you also need the good you have from your servant, that he may help you.
8.295	You cannot draw water for yourself, cannot cook for yourself, cannot run before your horse, cannot tend your beast.
8.296	You see that you need the good you have from your servant; you need his service.
8.297	Therefore you are not a true lord, while you have need of an inferior.
8.298	He is the true Lord, who seeks nothing from us; and woe to us if we do not seek Him!
8.299	He seeks nothing from us; yet He sought us when we did not seek Him.
8.300	One sheep had strayed; He found it, He brought it back on His shoulders rejoicing.
8.301	And was the sheep necessary for the Shepherd, and not rather the Shepherd necessary for the sheep? The more I love to speak of charity, the less willing I am that this epistle should be finished.
8.302	No one is more ardent in commending charity.
8.303	Nothing sweeter is preached to you, nothing more wholesome is drunk by you; but only if by godly living you confirm in yourselves the gift of God.
8.304	Do not be ungrateful for His so great grace, who, though He had one Only Son, would not have Him be a Son alone; but, that He might have brethren, adopted to Himself those who should possess eternal life with Him.
T:9	Homily IX (1 John 4:17–21)
9.1	“In this love is made perfect in us, that we may have boldness in the day of judgment; because as He is, so are we in this world.
9.2	There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear, because fear has torment.
9.3	He who fears is not made perfect in love.
9.4	Let us love Him, because He first loved us.
9.5	If someone says, ‘I love God,’ and hates his brother, he is a liar; for he who does not love his brother whom he sees, how can he love God whom he does not see?
9.6	And this commandment we have from Him: that he who loves God should love his brother also.”
9.7	1. You remember, beloved, that of the epistles of John the apostle the last part remains to be handled by us and expounded to you, as the Lord grants.
9.8	Of this debt, then, we are mindful; and you ought to be mindful of your claim.
9.9	For indeed this same charity, which in this epistle is chiefly and almost alone commended, at once makes us most faithful in paying our debts, and you most sweet in exacting your rights.
9.10	I have said “most sweet in exacting,” because where charity is not, he who exacts is bitter; but where charity is, both he who exacts is sweet, and he from whom it is exacted, although he undertakes some labor, yet charity makes the very labor almost no labor, and light.
9.11	Do we not see how, even in dumb and irrational animals, where the love is not spiritual but carnal and natural, the mother yields herself with great affection to her young ones when they would have the milk which is their right; and however impetuously the suckling rushes at the teats, yet that is better for the mother than that it should not suck nor exact what is due from love?
9.12	Often we see great calves driving their heads at the cow’s udders with a force that almost lifts up the mother’s body, yet she does not kick them off; indeed, if the young one is not there to suck, the lowing of the mother calls for it to come to the teats.
9.13	If, then, there is in us that spiritual charity of which the apostle says, “I became small in your midst, even as a nurse cherishing her young ones,” we love you the more when you are exacting.
9.14	We do not like the sluggish, because for the languid ones we are afraid.
9.15	We have been obliged, however, to interrupt the continuous reading of this epistle, because of certain appointed lessons coming in between, which had to be read on their holy days, and preached upon as well.
9.16	Let us now come back to the order which was interrupted; and what remains, holy brethren, receive with all attention.
9.17	I do not know whether charity could be more magnificently commended to us than by its being said, “Charity is God.”
9.18	Brief praise, yet mighty praise: brief in utterance, mighty in meaning!
9.19	How quickly it is said, “Love is God!”
9.20	This also is short: if you count it, it is one; if you weigh it, how great it is!
9.21	“Love is God, and he who dwells,” he says, “in love dwells in God, and God dwells in him.”
9.22	Let God be your house, and be yourself a house of God; dwell in God, and let God dwell in you.
9.23	God dwells in you that He may hold you; you dwell in God that you may not fall; for thus the apostle says of this same charity, “Charity never falls.”
9.24	How should he fall whom God holds?
9.25	2. “In this our love is made perfect in us, that we may have boldness in the day of judgment; because as He is, so are we in this world.”
9.26	He tells how each may test himself, what progress charity has made in him, or rather what progress he has made in charity.
9.27	For if charity is God, God is capable neither of progress nor of decline: that charity is said to be making progress in you means only that you are making progress in it.
9.28	Ask, therefore, what progress you have made in charity, and what your heart will answer you, that you may know the measure of your progress.
9.29	For he has promised to show us by what we may know Him, and has said, “In this love is made perfect in us.”
9.30	Ask: in what? “That we may have boldness in the day of judgment.”
9.31	Whoever has boldness in the day of judgment, in that man charity is made perfect.
9.32	What is it to have boldness in the day of judgment?
9.33	Not to fear that the day of judgment should come.
9.34	There are men who do not believe in a day of judgment; these cannot have boldness in a day which they do not believe will come.
9.35	Let us pass these by: may God awaken them, that they may live; why do we speak of the dead?
9.36	They do not believe that there will be a day of judgment; they neither fear nor desire what they do not believe.
9.37	A man has begun to believe in a day of judgment: if he has begun to believe, he has also begun to fear.
9.38	But because he still fears, because he does not yet have boldness in the day of judgment, charity is not yet made perfect in that man.
9.39	But for all that, should one despair?
9.40	In one in whom you see the beginning, why do you despair of the end?
9.41	“What beginning do I see?” you say.
9.42	That very fear. Hear the Scripture: “The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.”
9.43	Well then, he has begun to fear the day of judgment: by fearing let him correct himself, let him watch against his enemies, that is, his sins; let him begin to come to life again inwardly, and to put to death his members which are on the earth, as the apostle says, “Put to death your members which are on the earth.”
9.44	By the members on earth he means spiritual wickedness; for he goes on to explain it: “Covetousness, uncleanness,” and the rest which he lists there.
9.45	Now in proportion as this man who has begun to fear the day of judgment puts to death his members which are on the earth, in that proportion the heavenly members rise up and are strengthened.
9.46	But the heavenly members are all good works.
9.47	As the heavenly members rise up, he begins to desire what he once feared.
9.48	Once he feared lest Christ should come and find in him the ungodly man whom He must condemn; now he longs for Him to come, because He will find the godly man whom He may crown.
9.49	Having now begun to desire Christ’s coming, the chaste soul which desires the embrace of the Bridegroom renounces the adulterer and becomes a virgin within by faith, hope, and charity.
9.50	Now the man has boldness in the day of judgment: he does not fight against himself when he prays, “Your kingdom come.”
9.51	For he who fears lest the kingdom of God should come fears lest his prayer be heard.
9.52	How can he be said to pray who fears lest his prayer be heard?
9.53	But he who prays with the boldness of charity wishes now that He may come.
9.54	Of this same desire one said in the Psalm, “And You, O Lord, how long?
9.55	Turn, O Lord, and deliver my soul.”
9.56	He groaned at being so put off.
9.57	For there are men who with patience submit to die; but there are some perfect ones who with patience endure to live.
9.58	What do I mean?
9.59	When a person still desires this life, that person, when the day of death comes, patiently endures death: he struggles against himself that he may follow the will of God, and in his mind desires what God chooses, not what man’s will chooses; from desire of the present life there comes a reluctance toward death, but yet he takes to himself patience and fortitude, that he may meet death with an even mind; he dies patiently.
9.60	But when a man desires, as the apostle says, “to be dissolved and to be with Christ,” that person does not die patiently, but lives patiently and dies with delight.
9.61	See the apostle living patiently, that is, how with patience he here does not love life but endures it.
9.62	“To be dissolved,” he says, “and to be with Christ is far better; but to remain in the flesh is necessary for your sakes.”
9.63	Therefore, brethren, make every effort; settle it inwardly within yourselves to make this your concern, that you may desire the day of judgment.
9.64	In no other way is charity proved to be perfect than when one has begun to desire that day.
9.65	But that man desires it who has boldness in it, whose conscience feels no alarm, in perfect and sincere charity.
9.66	3. “In this His love is perfected in us, that we may have boldness in the day of judgment.”
9.67	Why shall we have boldness?
9.68	“Because as He is, so are we also in this world.”
9.69	You have heard the ground of your boldness: “Because as He is,” says the apostle, “so are we also in this world.”
9.70	Does he not seem to have said something impossible?
9.71	For is it possible for man to be as God?
9.72	I have already explained to you that “as” is not always said of equality, but is said of a certain resemblance.
9.73	For how do you say, “As I have ears, so has my image”?
9.74	Is it quite so? And yet you say “so, as.”
9.75	If, then, we were made after God’s image, why are we not as God?
9.76	Not to the point of equality, but in proportion to our measure.
9.77	From where, then, are we given boldness in the day of judgment?
9.78	“Because as He is, so are we also in this world.”
9.79	We must refer this to the same charity, and understand what is meant.
9.80	The Lord says in the Gospel, “If you love those who love you, what reward will you have? Do not the tax collectors do this?”
9.81	Then what would He have us do?
9.82	“But I say to you, Love your enemies, and pray for those who persecute you.”
9.83	If, then, He bids us love our enemies, from where does He bring an example to set before us?
9.84	From God Himself; for He says, “That you may be the children of your Father who is in heaven.”
9.85	How does God do this?
9.86	He loves His enemies, He “who makes His sun rise on the good and the bad, and sends rain on the just and the unjust.”
9.87	If, then, this is the perfection to which God invites us, that we love our enemies as He loved His, this is our boldness in the day of judgment, that “as He is, so are we also in this world”; because, as He loves His enemies in making His sun rise on good and bad, and in sending rain on the just and unjust, so we, since we cannot bestow sun and rain on them, bestow on them our tears when we pray for them.
9.88	4. Now, therefore, concerning this same boldness, let us see what he says.
9.89	How do we understand that charity is perfect?
9.90	“There is no fear in charity.”
9.91	Then what do we say of him who has begun to fear the day of judgment?
9.92	If charity in him were perfect, he would not fear.
9.93	For perfect charity would make perfect righteousness, and he would have nothing to fear; rather, he would have something to desire: that iniquity may pass away, and God’s kingdom come.
9.94	So then, “there is no fear in charity.”
9.95	But in what charity?
9.96	Not in charity begun. In what, then?
9.97	“But perfect charity,” he says, “casts out fear.”
9.98	Then let fear make the beginning, because “the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.”
9.99	Fear, so to speak, prepares a place for charity.
9.100	But once charity has begun to dwell there, the fear which prepared the place for it is cast out.
9.101	For in proportion as this increases, that decreases; and the more this comes to be within, the more fear is cast out.
9.102	Greater charity, less fear; less charity, greater fear.
9.103	But if there is no fear, there is no way for charity to come in.
9.104	As we see in sewing, the thread is brought in by means of the bristle: the bristle enters first, but unless it comes out, the thread does not come into its place; so fear first occupies the mind, but the fear does not remain there, because it enters only in order to bring in charity.
9.105	Once there is a sense of security in the mind, what joy we have, both in this world and in the world to come!
9.106	Even in this world, who shall hurt us, being full of charity?
9.107	See how the apostle exults concerning this very charity: “Who shall separate us from the charity of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?”
9.108	And Peter says: “And who is he who will harm you, if you are followers of what is good?—There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear, because fear has torment.”
9.109	The consciousness of sins torments the heart: justification has not yet taken place.
9.110	There is something in it that itches, that pricks.
9.111	Accordingly, in the Psalm, what does he say concerning this same perfection of righteousness?
9.112	“You have turned my mourning into joy for me; You have put off my sackcloth and girded me with gladness, to the end that my glory may sing to You, and that I may not be pricked.”
9.113	What is this, “That I may not be pricked”?
9.114	That there may not be anything that will goad my conscience.
9.115	Fear goads; but do not fear: charity enters in, and she heals the wound that fear inflicts.
9.116	The fear of God wounds as the physician’s knife does; it takes away the rottenness, and seems to make the wound greater.
9.117	Behold, when the rottenness was in the body, the wound was smaller, but dangerous; then comes the knife; the wound hurt less then than it hurts now while the physician is cutting it.
9.118	It hurts more while he is operating on it than it would if it were not operated on; it hurts more under the healing operation, but only so that it may never hurt once the healing is accomplished.
9.119	Then let fear occupy your heart, that it may bring in charity; let the scar follow the physician’s knife.
9.120	He is such a Healer that the scars do not even appear; only put yourself under His hand.
9.121	For if you are without fear, you cannot be justified.
9.122	It is a sentence pronounced by the Scriptures: “For he who is without fear cannot be justified.”
9.123	Fear, then, must enter first, that by it charity may come.
9.124	Fear is the healing operation; charity, the sound condition.
9.125	“But he who fears is not made perfect in love.”
9.126	Why? “Because fear has torment,” just as the cutting of the surgeon’s knife has torment.
9.127	5. But there is another saying which seems contrary to this, if it does not have someone who understands it.
9.128	Namely, it is said in a certain place in the Psalms, “The fear of the Lord is chaste, enduring forever.”
9.129	He shows us an eternal fear, but a chaste one.
9.130	But if he there shows us an eternal fear, does this epistle perhaps contradict him when it says, “There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear”?
9.131	Let us question both utterances of God.
9.132	The Spirit is one, though the books are two, though the mouths are two, though the tongues are two.
9.133	For this is said by the mouth of John, that by the mouth of David; but do not think that the Spirit is more than one.
9.134	If one breath fills two pipes [of the double flute], cannot one Spirit fill two hearts and move two tongues?
9.135	But if two pipes filled by one breathing sound in unison, can two tongues filled with the Spirit or Breath of God make a discord?
9.136	There is, then, a unison there, there is a harmony; only it requires someone who can hear.
9.137	Behold, this Spirit of God has breathed into and filled two hearts, has moved two tongues; and we have heard from the one tongue, “There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear”; we have heard from the other, “The fear of the Lord is chaste, enduring forever.”
9.138	How is this? The notes seem to jar.
9.139	Not so: rouse your ears; mark the melody.
9.140	It is not without cause that in the one place the word “chaste” is added, and in the other it is not added; but it is because there is one fear which is called chaste, and there is another fear which is not called chaste.
9.141	Let us mark the difference between these two fears, and so understand the harmony of the flutes.
9.142	How are we to understand, or how to distinguish?
9.143	Mark, my beloved. There are men who fear God lest they be cast into hell, lest perhaps they burn with the devil in everlasting fire.
9.144	This is the fear which brings in charity; but it comes in order to depart.
9.145	For if you still fear God because of punishments, you do not yet love Him whom you fear in this way.
9.146	You do not desire the good things, but are afraid of the evil things.
9.147	Yet because you are afraid of the evil things, you correct yourself and begin to desire the good things.
9.148	Once you have begun to desire the good, there will be in you the chaste fear.
9.149	What is the chaste fear?
9.150	The fear lest you lose the good things themselves.
9.151	Mark! It is one thing to fear God lest He cast you into hell with the devil, and another thing to fear God lest He forsake you.
9.152	The fear by which you fear lest you be cast into hell with the devil is not yet chaste, for it does not come from the love of God but from the fear of punishment; but when you fear God lest His presence forsake you, you embrace Him, you long to enjoy God Himself.
9.153	6. One cannot better explain the difference between these two fears, the one which charity casts out, the other chaste, which endures forever, than by putting the case of two married women, one of whom, you may suppose, is willing to commit adultery, delights in wickedness, and only fears lest she be condemned by her husband.
9.154	She fears her husband; but because she still loves wickedness, that is the reason why she fears her husband.
9.155	To this woman the presence of her husband is not welcome but burdensome; and if she happens to live wickedly, she fears her husband, lest he should come.
9.156	Such are those who fear the coming of the day of judgment.
9.157	Put the case that the other loves her husband, that she feels she owes him chaste embraces, that she stains herself with no uncleanness of adultery: she wishes for the presence of her husband.
9.158	And how are these two fears distinguished?
9.159	The one woman fears, the other also fears.
9.160	Question them: they seem to give one answer. Question the one: “Do you fear your husband?” She answers, “I do.”
9.161	Question the other, whether she fears her husband; she answers, “I do fear him.”
9.162	The voice is one, the mind different.
9.163	Now then let them be questioned: “Why?”
9.164	The one says, “I fear my husband, lest he should come”; the other says, “I fear my husband, lest he depart from me.”
9.165	The one says, “I fear to be condemned”; the other, “I fear to be forsaken.”
9.166	Let the same take place in the mind of Christians, and you find a fear which love casts out, and another fear, chaste, enduring forever.

0.1	“Then Jesus said, Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.” — Luke 23:34.
0.2	Our Lord was at that moment enduring the first pains of crucifixion; the executioners had just then driven the nails through his hands and feet.
0.3	Moreover, he must have been greatly depressed and brought into a state of extreme weakness by the agony of the night in Gethsemane, and by the scourgings and cruel mockings he had endured all through the morning from Caiaphas, Pilate, Herod, and the Praetorian guards.
0.4	Yet neither the weakness of the past nor the pain of the present could keep him from continuing in prayer.
0.5	The Lamb of God was silent to men, but he was not silent to God.
0.6	Silent as a sheep before her shearers, he had not a word to say to man in his own defense, but in his heart he continues crying to his Father, and no pain and no weakness can silence his holy supplications.
0.7	Beloved, what an example our Lord sets before us here!
0.8	Let us continue in prayer as long as our heart beats; let no excess of suffering drive us away from the throne of grace, but rather let it drive us closer to it.
0.9	“As long as they live, Christians should pray, / For only while they pray do they live.”
0.10	To stop praying is to give up the comforts that our condition requires.
0.11	Under all distractions of spirit and all overwhelmings of heart, great God, help us still to pray, and may despair never drive our footsteps from the mercy seat.
0.12	Our blessed Redeemer persevered in prayer even when the cruel iron tore his tender nerves, and blow after blow of the hammer shook his whole frame with anguish; and this perseverance may be explained by the fact that he was so much in the habit of prayer that he could not stop; he had gained so mighty a momentum of intercession that it would not allow him to pause.
0.13	Those long nights on the cold mountainside, those many days spent in solitude, those constant brief prayers he used to send darting up to heaven—all these had formed in him a habit so powerful that the severest torments could not check its force.
0.14	Yet it was more than habit.
0.15	Our Lord was baptized in the spirit of prayer; he lived in it, it lived in him, it had become an element of his nature.
0.16	He was like that precious spice which, when bruised, does not stop giving off its perfume, but rather yields it all the more abundantly because of the blows of the pestle, its fragrance being no outward and superficial quality, but an inward virtue essential to its nature, which the pounding in the mortar only drew out of it, causing it to reveal its secret soul of sweetness.
0.17	So Jesus prays, just as a bundle of myrrh gives off its scent, or as birds sing because they cannot do otherwise.
0.18	Prayer wrapped his very soul like a garment, and his heart went forth arrayed in it.
0.19	I repeat it: let this be our example—never, under any circumstances, however severe the trial or depressing the difficulty, let us stop praying.
0.20	Notice further that our Lord, in the prayer before us, remains in the full vigor of faith regarding his Sonship.
0.21	The extreme trial to which he now submitted himself could not keep him from holding fast to his Sonship.
0.22	His prayer begins, “Father.”
0.23	It was not without meaning that he taught us to say, when we pray, “Our Father,” for our success in prayer will depend greatly on our confidence in our relationship to God.
0.24	Under great losses and crosses, we are apt to think that God is not dealing with us as a father with a child, but rather as a severe judge with a condemned criminal; but the cry of Christ, when he is brought to an extremity we shall never reach, shows no faltering in the spirit of sonship.
0.25	In Gethsemane, when the bloody sweat fell fast upon the ground, his bitterest cry began with “My Father,” asking that, if it were possible, the cup of gall might pass from him; he pleaded with the Lord as his Father, just as he had called him over and over again on that dark and dismal night.
0.26	Here again, in this, the first of his seven dying cries, it is “Father.”
0.27	Oh, that the Spirit who makes us cry, “Abba, Father,” may never cease his working!
0.28	May we never be brought into spiritual bondage by the suggestion, “If you are the Son of God”; or if the tempter should attack us in that way, may we triumph as Jesus did in the wilderness when he was hungry.
0.29	May the Spirit who cries, “Abba, Father,” drive back every unbelieving fear.
0.30	When we are chastened, as we must be (for what son is there that his father does not chasten?), may we be in loving submission to the Father of our spirits, and live; but may we never become captives to the spirit of bondage, so as to doubt the love of our gracious Father or our share in his adoption.

0.32	It is true that he continued to pray for himself on the cross, and his mournful cry, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” shows the personal side of his prayer; but the first of the seven great cries on the cross has scarcely even an indirect reference to himself.
0.33	It is, “Father, forgive them.”
0.34	The petition is entirely for others, and though there is an allusion to the cruelties they were inflicting on him, it is remote; and you will notice that he does not say, “I forgive them”—that is taken for granted—he seems to lose sight of the fact that they were doing him any wrong; it is the wrong they were doing to the Father that is on his mind, the insult they are offering to the Father in the person of the Son; he does not think of himself at all.
0.35	The cry, “Father, forgive them,” is completely unselfish.
0.36	In the prayer he himself is as though he were not; so complete is his self-annihilation that he loses sight of himself and his sorrows.
0.37	My brothers, if there had ever been a time in the life of the Son of Man when he might have strictly confined his prayer to himself without anyone finding fault with it, surely it was when he was beginning his death throes.
0.38	We could not wonder, if any man here were fastened to the stake or fixed to a cross, if his first, and even his last and all his prayers, were for support under so severe a trial.
0.39	But see, the Lord Jesus began his prayer by pleading for others.
0.40	Do you not see what a great heart is revealed here!
0.41	What a soul of compassion was in the Crucified One!
0.42	How Godlike, how divine!
0.43	Was there ever anyone like him before, who, even in the very pangs of death, offers as his first prayer an intercession for others?
0.44	Let this unselfish spirit be in you also, my brothers.
0.45	Let no man look on his own things alone, but let every man also look on the things of others.
0.46	Love your neighbors as yourselves, and since Christ has set before you this paragon of unselfishness, seek to follow him, treading in his steps.
0.47	There is, however, a crowning jewel in this diadem of glorious love.

0.49	His enemies, did I say? There is more than that to be considered.
0.50	It was not a prayer for enemies who had done him an evil deed years before, but for those who were there and then murdering him.
0.51	The Savior did not pray in cold blood, after he had forgotten the injury and could the more easily forgive it, but while the first red drops of blood were spurting on the hands that drove the nails; while the hammer was still stained with crimson gore, his blessed mouth poured out the fresh, warm prayer, “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.”
0.52	I do not say that that prayer was confined to his immediate executioners.

0.54	How sublime this prayer is when viewed in such a light!
0.55	It stands alone upon a mountain of solitary glory.
0.56	No other prayer had been prayed like it before.
0.57	It is true that Abraham, and Moses, and the prophets had prayed for the wicked; but not for wicked men who had pierced their hands and feet.
0.58	It is true that Christians since that day have offered the same prayer, just as Stephen cried, “Do not lay this sin to their charge”; and many a martyr has made his last words at the stake words of pitying intercession for his persecutors; but you know where they learned this—let me ask you, where did he learn it?
0.59	Was not Jesus the divine original?
0.60	He learned it nowhere; it sprang up from his own Godlike nature.
0.61	A compassion unique to himself dictated this originality of prayer; the inward royalty of his love suggested to him so memorable an intercession, which may serve us as a pattern, but for which no pattern had existed before.
0.62	I feel as though I could better kneel before my Lord’s cross at this moment than stand in this pulpit to speak to you.
0.63	I want to adore him; I worship him in my heart for that prayer; if I knew nothing else of him but this one prayer, I must adore him, for that one matchless plea for mercy convinces me most overwhelmingly of the deity of him who offered it, and fills my heart with reverent affection.
0.64	Thus I have introduced to you our Lord’s first spoken prayer upon the cross.
0.65	I shall now, if we are helped by God’s Holy Spirit, make some use of it.
0.66	First, we shall view it as illustrative of our Savior’s intercession; secondly, we shall regard the text as instructive regarding the church’s work; thirdly, we shall consider it as suggestive to the unconverted.
H:0.8	I. First, my dear brothers, let us look at this very wonderful text as ILLUSTRATIVE OF OUR LORD’S INTERCESSION.
0.67	He prayed for his enemies then; he is praying for his enemies now; the past on the cross was a pledge of the present on the throne.
0.68	He is in a higher place and in a nobler condition, but his occupation is the same; he still continues before the eternal throne to present pleas on behalf of guilty men, crying, “Father, O forgive them.”
0.69	All his intercession is in some measure like the intercession on Calvary, and Calvary’s cries may help us to guess the character of the whole of his intercession above.
0.70	The first point in which we may see the character of his intercession is this—it is most gracious.
0.71	Those for whom our Lord prayed, according to the text, did not deserve his prayer.
0.72	They had done nothing that could call forth a blessing from him as a reward for their efforts in his service; on the contrary, they were most undeserving persons, who had conspired to put him to death.
0.73	They had crucified him, crucified him wantonly and maliciously; they were even then taking away his innocent life.
0.74	His clients were persons who, far from being meritorious, were utterly undeserving of a single good wish from the Savior’s heart.
0.75	They certainly never asked him to pray for them—it was the last thought in their minds to say, “Intercede for us, O dying King!
0.76	Offer petitions on our behalf, O Son of God!”
0.77	I will venture to believe that the prayer itself, when they heard it, was either disregarded and passed over with contemptuous indifference, or perhaps seized upon as a subject for jest.
0.78	I admit that it seems too severe upon humanity to suppose it possible that such a prayer could have been a subject for laughter, and yet there were other things done around the cross which were quite as brutal, and I can imagine that this also might have happened.
0.79	Yet our Savior prayed for persons who did not deserve the prayer, but, on the contrary, deserved a curse—persons who did not ask for the prayer, and even scoffed at it when they heard it.
0.80	Even so, in heaven there stands the great High Priest, who pleads for guilty men—for guilty men, my hearers.
0.81	There are none on earth who deserve his intercession.
0.82	He pleads for none on the supposition that they do deserve it.
0.83	He stands there to plead as the Just One on behalf of the unjust.
0.84	Not if any man is righteous, but “if any man sins, we have an advocate with the Father.”
0.85	Remember, too, that our great Intercessor pleads for those who never asked him to plead for them.
0.86	His elect, while still dead in trespasses and sins, are the objects of his compassionate intercessions, and while they even scoff at his gospel, his heart of love is entreating the favor of heaven on their behalf.

0.88	Some of you, with many tears and much earnestness, have been beseeching the Savior to be your advocate?
0.89	Will he refuse you?
0.90	Does it stand to reason that he can?
0.91	He pleads for those who reject his pleadings; how much more for you who prize them above gold.
0.92	Remember, my dear hearer, if there is nothing good in you, and if there is everything conceivable that is malignant and bad, yet none of these things can be any barrier to prevent Christ from exercising the office of Intercessor for you.
0.93	Even for you he will plead.
0.94	Come, put your case into his hands; for you he will find pleas which you cannot discover for yourselves, and he will put the case to God for you as for his murderers, “Father, forgive them.”
0.95	A second quality of his intercession is this—its careful spirit.
0.96	You notice in the prayer, “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.”
0.97	Our Savior, as it were, looked his enemies through and through to find something in them that he could urge in their favor; but he could see nothing until his wisely affectionate eye lighted upon their ignorance: “they do not know what they do.”
0.98	How carefully he surveyed the circumstances and the characters of those for whom he pleaded so urgently!
0.99	It is just so with him in heaven.
0.100	Christ is no careless advocate for his people.
0.101	He knows your precise condition at this moment, and the exact state of your heart with regard to the temptation through which you are passing; more than that, he foresees the temptation that awaits you, and in his intercession he takes note of the future event which his foreseeing eye beholds.
0.102	“Satan has desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat; but I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail.”
0.103	Oh, the condescending tenderness of our great High Priest!
0.104	He knows us better than we know ourselves.
0.105	He understands every secret grief and groaning.
0.106	You need not trouble yourself about the wording of your prayer; he will put the wording right.
0.107	And even if you should fail in understanding the exact petition, he cannot, for as he knows what is the mind of God, so he knows what is your mind also.
0.108	He can spy out some reason for mercy in you which you cannot detect in yourselves, and when it is so dark and cloudy with your soul that you cannot discern a foothold for a plea that you may urge with heaven, the Lord Jesus has the pleas ready framed and the petitions ready drawn up, and he can present them acceptably before the mercy seat.

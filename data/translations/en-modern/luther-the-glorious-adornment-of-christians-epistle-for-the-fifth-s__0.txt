0.1	Text: Colossians 3:12-17.
0.2	12 Put on therefore, as God's elect, holy and beloved, a heart of compassion, kindness, lowliness, meekness, longsuffering; 13 bearing with one another and forgiving each other, if anyone has a complaint against anyone; just as the Lord forgave you, so must you also do. 14 And above all these things put on love, which is the bond of perfectness.
0.3	15 And let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts, for to this you were also called in one body; and be thankful.
0.4	16 Let the Word of Christ dwell in you richly; in all wisdom teaching and admonishing one another with psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with grace in your hearts to God.
0.5	17 And whatever you do, in word or in deed, do it all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.
0.6	1. This text is also a letter of admonition, teaching what kind of fruit properly results from faith.
0.7	Paul deals kindly with the Colossians.
0.8	He does not command, urge or threaten, as teachers of the Law must do with those who are under the Law.
0.9	He persuades them with loving words in view of the blessing and grace of God they have received, and in the light of Christ's own example.
0.10	Christians should act with readiness and cheerfulness, moved neither by fear of punishment nor by desire for reward, as has often been stated before.
0.11	This admonition has been repeated so often in the preceding epistle lesson that we know, I trust, what makes a Christian.
0.12	Therefore we will only touch briefly on the subject.
0.13	“Put on, therefore.”
0.14	2. In the epistle for New Year's Day we have sufficiently explained the meaning of “putting on”: how by faith we put on Christ, and he puts on us; how in love we put on our neighbor, and our neighbor puts on us.
0.15	The Christian's clothing is of two kinds—faith and love.
0.16	Christ wore two kinds of garments—one whole and representing faith, the other divided and representing love.
0.17	Paul here refers to the latter garment, love.
0.18	He would teach us Christians what kind of ornaments and apparel we are to wear in the world—not silk or precious gold.
0.19	These are forbidden to women by Peter (1 Peter 3:3) and by Paul (1 Timothy 2:9).
0.20	Love for our neighbor is a garment that suits us well—that love which leads us to concern ourselves with our neighbor and his misfortunes.
0.21	Such love is called the ornament of Christian character—an ornament in the eyes of men.
0.22	3. Notice the tender and sacred manner of the apostle's admonition, a manner he is accustomed to use toward us.
0.23	He does not drive us with laws, but persuades us by reminding us of the inexpressible grace of God; for he calls us the “elect of God,” and “holy” and “beloved.”
0.24	He would call forth the fruits of faith, desiring that they be yielded in a willing, cheerful and happy spirit.
0.25	The person who sincerely believes and trusts that before God he is beloved, holy and elect will consider how to maintain his honors and titles and how to conduct himself worthily of them; more than that, he will love God with such fervor that he can do or leave undone, or suffer, all things cheerfully, and he will never know how to do enough.
0.26	But he who doubts that God is so disposed toward him will not recognize the force of these words.
0.27	He will not feel the power of the statement that we are holy, beloved and elect in the sight of God.
0.28	4. Let us disregard, therefore, the saints who elect and love themselves; who adorn themselves with the works of the Law; who observe fasts and disciplines; who pay regard to clothing and position—for they are unwilling to be sinners before God.
0.29	Our ornaments are unlike these and have nothing to do with such mockeries.
0.30	They are honesty, sincerity, good works and service to our neighbor.
0.31	We are not bound by laws regarding food, clothing, times and the like.
0.32	We are holy in the sight of God, before whom no one can be holy until he sees himself as a sinner and rejects his own righteousness.
0.33	But the people just mentioned are holy in their own estimation; therefore they always remain wicked—sinners in the sight of God.
0.34	We are beloved of God because we despise ourselves; we judge and condemn ourselves and reject our self-love.
0.35	The others, because they love and esteem themselves, are despicable and unacceptable in the sight of God.
0.36	Again, we are chosen by God for the reason that we despise ourselves as filth.
0.37	Such people God chooses, and has chosen from eternity.
0.38	Because the would-be saints elect themselves, God will reject them, as indeed he has from eternity.
0.39	Now, this is what Paul means by these words:
0.40	“A heart of compassion.”
0.41	5. These words stand for part of the ornament, the beautiful, charming Christian jewel, which suits us better in the sight of God than pearls, precious stones, silk and gold suit us in the eyes of the world.
0.42	“A heart of compassion” is evidence of the true Christian.
0.43	Paul would say: “You are to be merciful not simply in outward deed or in appearance, but in the inmost heart.”
0.44	He refers to that sincere and wholehearted mercy found in the father and mother who see the distress of a child for whom they would readily risk their lives or give up all they possess.
0.45	The Christian's mind and heart should be constantly devoted to merciful deeds, with an ardor so intense that he is unaware he is doing good and compassionate acts.
0.46	6. With this single phrase Paul condemns the works and arbitrary rules of hypocritical saints, whose severity will not allow them to associate with sinners.
0.47	Their rigorous laws must control everything.
0.48	They do nothing but compel and drive.
0.49	They show no mercy, but only constant reproach, censure, condemnation, blame and bluster.
0.50	They can endure no imperfection.
0.51	But among Christians many are sinners, many are weak.
0.52	In fact, Christians associate only with these, not with saints.
0.53	Christians reject no one, but bear with all.
0.54	Indeed, they are as sincerely concerned for sinners as they would be for themselves if they were the weak ones.
0.55	They pray for sinners, teach, admonish, persuade, and do all in their power to win them back.
0.56	Such is the true character of a Christian.
0.57	So God, in Christ, has dealt with us and always deals with us.
0.58	So Christ dealt with the adulteress (John 8:11) when he set her free from her tormentors, and with his gracious words moved her to repentance and let her go.
0.59	We read that St. Antony said Paphrutius knew how souls are to be saved, because he rescued a certain man from brothers who persecuted and oppressed him for his transgression.
0.60	See the “Lives of the Fathers.”
0.61	If God were to deal with us according to the strictness of his laws, we would all be lost.
0.62	But he mercifully suspends the Law.
0.63	Isaiah says (9:4): “For the yoke of his burden, and the staff of his shoulder, the rod of his oppressor, you have broken.”
0.64	God now only persuades.
0.65	7. Note how entangled in the Law and in hypocrisy they still are who consider themselves eminent saints and at the same time are intolerant of the weaknesses of Christians.
0.66	If they fail to find perfect holiness—a miracle of purity—in those who possess Christ and know the Gospel, then nothing is as it should be; the heavens are about to fall and the earth about to be destroyed.
0.67	They can only judge, censure and mock, saying: “Oh, yes, he is truly evangelical; indeed, he is a visionary!”
0.68	In this way they reveal their utter blindness.
0.69	With the beam constantly in their own eyes, they show how little they know of Christ.
0.70	Know, then, when you meet someone so ready to censure and condemn, someone who demands absolute perfection in Christians—know that such a person is merely an enforcer of the Law, a base hypocrite, a merciless jailer, with no true knowledge of Christ.
0.71	Just as among Christians there is no law but all is love, so neither can there be judgment, condemnation and censure.
0.72	And he who calls another a visionary is certainly ten times a visionary himself.
0.73	In the very thing for which he judges and condemns another, he condemns himself.
0.74	Since he ignores mercy and everything but the Law, he finds no mercy in the sight of God; in fact, he has never experienced, never tasted, God's mercy.
0.75	To his taste, both God and neighbor are as bitter as gall and wormwood.
0.76	8. But tender mercy is to be shown only to Christians and only among Christians.
0.77	With those who reject and persecute the Gospel we must deal differently.
0.78	It is not right that my charity should be so liberal as to tolerate unsound doctrine.
0.79	In the case of false faith and doctrine there is neither love nor patience.
0.80	Against these it is my duty to contend earnestly and not to yield a hair's breadth.
0.81	Otherwise—when faith is not endangered—I must be unfailingly kind and merciful to all, notwithstanding the weaknesses of their lives.
0.82	I may not censure, oppress or drive; I must persuade, entreat and tolerate.
0.83	A defective life does not destroy Christianity; it exercises it.
0.84	But defective doctrine—false belief—destroys all good.
0.85	So, then, toleration and mercy are not permissible in the case of unsound doctrine; only anger, opposition and death are in order, yet always in accordance with the Word of God.
0.86	9. On the other hand, those who are mercifully tolerated must not imagine that, because they escape censure and force, their beliefs and practices are right.
0.87	They must not take such mercy as encouragement to become lazy and negligent and to continue in their error.
0.88	Mercy is not extended to them with any such intention.
0.89	The purpose is to give them opportunity to recover zeal and strength.
0.90	But if they are inclined to remain as they are, very well; let them alone.
0.91	They will not continue so for long; the devil will lead them farther astray, until finally they fall away completely and even become enemies of the Gospel.
0.92	Such will be their end if they allow mercy to be lavished on them in vain.
0.93	We may not be lazy and asleep in the matter of our false doctrines, relying on the fact that we are not despised or constrained by men.
0.94	There is particular need to be active and diligent, for the devil neither sleeps nor rests.
0.95	We need to beware that he does not lead us where we will never enjoy God's mercy.
0.96	“Kindness, lowliness, meekness, longsuffering.”
0.97	10. These words represent the other elements of Christian character.
0.98	You will find kindness defined in the second epistle lesson for the early Christmas service.
0.99	It marks the conduct of the person who is gentle and sympathetic toward all, who repels no one with a forbidding face, harsh words or rude behavior.
0.100	We Germans would call such a person affable and friendly.
0.101	Kindness is a virtue not confined to certain works; it shapes the whole life.
0.102	The kind person is obliging to everyone, displeased with no one, and attractive to all people.
0.103	In contrast are those odd characters who take pleasure in nothing but their own notions; who insist that others adapt themselves to them and their ways, while they yield to no one.
0.104	Such people are called “uncivil.”
0.105	11. But the generosity of kindness is not to be extended to false doctrine.
0.106	It is to be exercised only in regard to conduct and works.
0.107	As has often been stated before, love with all its works and fruits has no place in the matter of unsound doctrine.
0.108	I must love my neighbor and show him kindness whatever the imperfections of his life.
0.109	But if he refuses to believe or to teach sound doctrine, I cannot, I dare not, love him or show him kindness.
0.110	According to Paul (Galatians 1:8-9), I must hold him excommunicated and accursed, even if he were an angel from heaven.
0.111	So remarkably do faith and love differ and stand apart.
0.112	Love will be, must be, kind even to the bitterest enemy so long as he does not assail faith and doctrine.
0.113	But it will not, it cannot, tolerate the person who does, be it father, mother or dearest friend.
0.114	Deuteronomy 13:6-8. Love, then, must be exercised not in relation to the doctrine and faith of our neighbor, but in relation to his life and works.
0.115	Faith, on the contrary, has to do not with his works and life, but with his doctrine and belief.
0.116	12. I think by this time we must know the meaning of “lowliness” of mind—esteeming oneself least and others greater.
0.117	Christ illustrates it by taking the lowest seat at the wedding, and doing so cheerfully.
0.118	We are to serve even when our service is not wanted, and to minister to our enemies.
0.119	So Christ humbled himself before Judas the betrayer, and before all of us.
0.120	He came not to be served, but to serve.
0.121	It is no wonder that humbleness of mind is a rare virtue, for every Christian grace is a rarity.
0.122	Graces are especially lacking among those who, professing to know most of Christ, find something to censure in all Christians.
0.123	Paul calls Christianity a mystery of God; and it is likely to remain one.
0.124	13. “Meekness” is the opposite of anger.
0.125	The meek person is not easily stirred to show anger, to curse, strike, hate or wish evil to anyone, even an enemy.
0.126	To refrain in this way is an art. Hypocrites—in fact, the whole world—can be meek toward friends and those who treat them well.
0.127	But true meekness and humility will remain only among the elect and beloved saints of God, as Paul implies here.
0.128	Even among these, many are lacking in all, or at least a large part, of the Christian graces.
0.129	So hypocrites may find something to censure, something to be offended at, in the beloved, elect saints of God.
0.130	And the true saints have occasion to exercise mercy, humility, meekness and forbearance.
0.131	Those whom Paul here calls elect and beloved saints of God, though somewhat lacking in humility, meekness and forbearance, are not therefore unholy, not rejected and despised.
0.132	14. Paul makes a distinction between longsuffering and forbearance, as in Romans 2:4: “Do you despise the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering?”
0.133	In “longsuffering” we have the thought expressed here and there by God in the Psalms and elsewhere by the Hebrew “arich apaim”—“slow to wrath.”
0.134	God patiently bears with evil.
0.135	Indeed, he repeatedly delays vengeance, apparently more ready to forgive than to punish, even under extreme provocation and when he has just reason to chastise.
0.136	Longsuffering reaches farther than patience.
0.137	Patience bears evil and injustice; but longsuffering delays punishment.
0.138	It does not intend to punish; it would not take hasty revenge.
0.139	Unlike the vengeful, it wishes no one evil.
0.140	We see many, indeed, who suffer much and are patient, but at the same time trust in a final revenge.
0.141	The longsuffering Christian, however, is opposed to revenge and desires the sinner to amend his ways.
0.142	“Bearing with one another, and forgiving each other, if anyone has a complaint against anyone; just as the Lord forgave you, so must you also do.”
0.143	15. In this verse all law is abolished among Christians.
0.144	No one is permitted to demand the recovery of his property through legal process.
0.145	He must forgive and yield.
0.146	Christ's example enjoins this principle; he has forgiven us.
0.147	And how far does his forgiveness extend?
0.148	He pardons past sins, but that is not all; as John says (1 John 2:1-2), “If anyone sins, we have an Advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous, and he is the propitiation for our sins.”
0.149	16. Note that it is the true Christian saints whom Paul describes, yet he regards them as weak to the point of offending and complaining against one another.
0.150	This is a state of affairs by no means fitting for Christians and saints.
0.151	So I say Christ's kingdom is a mystery too obscure for our preaching and teaching to explain sufficiently.
0.152	Unbelievers cannot be induced to work, but believers cannot be kept from working.
0.153	Some would not believe and some would not love.
0.154	It is true of Christ's kingdom that his Christians are not perfectly holy.
0.155	They have begun to be holy and are in a state of progress.
0.156	Among them are still found anger, evil desire, unholy love, worldly care and other deplorable weaknesses, remnants of the old Adam.
0.157	Paul speaks of these things as burdens which one must bear for a neighbor (Galatians 6:2), and in Romans 15:1 he admonishes us to “bear the infirmities of the weak.”
0.158	Likewise Christ loved his apostles much and suffered much from them, and he still bears with his own daily.
0.159	17. Some, listing the fruits of the Spirit mentioned in Galatians 5:22-23, say a Christian should be gentle, meek, longsuffering and chaste; and they regard this passage as a law commanding such fruits.
0.160	Hence they refuse to recognize as Christians any who fail to possess these fruits in perfection.
0.161	Now, such people cannot believe there is a Christ, certain as the fact is.
0.162	They judge maliciously, complaining that Christians do not exist.
0.163	They take offense at Christ for his superior wisdom.
0.164	For Christ has given us scriptural authority for knowing Christians by their fruits.
0.165	He says (Matthew 7:16), “By their fruits you shall know them.”
0.166	Here they are emphatic.
0.167	18. Can you locate where such a person fails?
0.168	He fails in this: he understands absolutely nothing of Christ's kingdom.
0.169	For he misinterprets the passages referring to Christians.
0.170	He takes the statement that Christians should be kind and meek to mean that they must never become angry, must bear anything and show impatience toward no one; if they do not do so, they cannot be Christians, for they do not have the fruits.
0.171	Dear man, what but his own blindness can lead him to such a conclusion?
0.172	He imagines Christianity to be a holy order of perfection, altogether without weakness, a perfection like that in heaven among the angels.
0.173	But tell me, where do the Scriptures speak of Christians in this way?
0.174	But whoever recognizes Christianity as a progressive order still in its beginning will not be offended at the occasional display of ungentleness, unkindness and impatience on the part of a Christian; for he remembers that Christians are commanded to bear one another's burdens and weaknesses.
0.175	He knows that the list of the fruits of the Spirit is not a record of laws that must be observed or else Christ will be denied.
0.176	He is aware that the passage is to be interpreted as meaning that Christians are to strive to be kind; that is the mark at which they aim.
0.177	However, even though they have made a beginning and some progress in this virtue, they are often unkind and bear fruits directly opposite to the fruits of the Spirit.
0.178	True, the text quoted says we should be kind, but it does not say we are kind.
0.179	We are tending toward it; we are in a state of progress; but during the progress much of the old and not yet transformed nature is mixed in.
0.180	19. Know, then, that in a mysterious way Christ is in his saints, and beware of judging or condemning anyone when you do not have positive assurance that he believes and teaches contrary to the Gospel.
0.181	But whoever does oppose the Gospel you may safely judge to be without Christ and under the sway of the devil.
0.182	Pray for such a person and admonish him, in the hope of his conversion.
0.183	But in the case of one who embraces and honors the Gospel, observe Paul's comment (Romans 14:4): “Who are you who judge the servant of another? To his own lord he stands or falls.
0.184	Yes, he shall be made to stand; for the Lord has power to make him stand.”
0.185	And again (1 Corinthians 10:12): “Therefore let the one who thinks he stands take heed that he does not fall.”
0.186	Christ would be at the same time hidden and revealed, found and not found.
0.187	He permits some weaknesses to be mixed with the fruits of the Spirit, so that he may conceal himself and so that malicious judges may be offended.
0.188	“And above all these things put on love, which is the bond of perfectness.”
0.189	20. From longsuffering and meekness the apostle distinguishes love and the other jewels of spiritual beauty of which we have already heard, though all are included in love.
0.190	As faith is the chief element of Christian character, so love is the chief of the fruits of the Spirit, the jewel of surpassing beauty.
0.191	Therefore Paul says, “Above all these things put on love.”
0.192	Love surpasses mercy, kindness, meekness and humility.
0.193	Paul calls it “the bond of perfectness” because it unites human hearts—not a partial unity, based on likeness or close kinship, but a complete unity among all people and in all relations.
0.194	It makes us of one mind, one heart, one desire.
0.195	It permits no one to start a peculiar order of doctrine or faith.
0.196	All who love hold the same belief.
0.197	Consequently there is the same purpose of heart with the poor and the rich, with rulers and subjects, the sick and the well, the high and the low, the honored and the disgraced.
0.198	The loving heart lets all share in its good; more than that, it shares in the adversities of all people, regarding them as its own.
0.199	Where love is, perfect unity and fellowship prevail in every event, good or bad.
0.200	It is a most perfect bond.
0.201	21. Where love is lacking, hearts are united and aims are one in only a few matters; in most things there is disagreement.
0.202	For instance: robbers have a common bond, but it is no more than a common purpose in committing robbery and murder.
0.203	Worldly friends are of one mind as far as their own interests are concerned.
0.204	Monks are united with regard to their order and their honor.
0.205	Herod and Pilate agreed, but only with regard to Christ.
0.206	For the most part it is rare that one monk, priest or layman agrees with another.
0.207	Their bond of union is weak; they are like chaff bound with straw.
0.208	“And let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts, for to this you were also called.”
0.209	22. There is much that threatens to break love's bond.
0.210	The devil never sleeps, but continually stirs up discord and unrest.
0.211	Paul does not deny that the bond is attacked.
0.212	But he urges us to resist, remembering that love must be exercised through opposition.
0.213	He admonishes us to let the peace of Christ have dominion in our hearts.
0.214	The thought of the verse is this: Though the peace of the world and the flesh does not last, though you must witness the forces of discord and disruption, nevertheless let your hearts have peace in Christ.
0.215	We spoke of the peace of God in the epistle selection for the Fourth Sunday in Advent—Philippians 4:7.
0.216	This is the peace to which the Gospel calls—not the peace of the world, the flesh or the devil, but the peace that surpasses all understanding, of which Paul tells us.
0.217	We are to hold on to the peace of God not only when all is well, but when sin, death, the flesh, the world and all calamities rage.
0.218	“And be thankful.”
0.219	23. “Thankfulness” here may be taken in either of two senses. First, thankfulness toward God, Paul's thought being: Let the remembrance of all God has done for you move you to gratitude for his grace and mercy, a gratitude which love and peace shall follow.
0.220	Secondly, we may understand thankfulness toward men—gratitude for all the benefits received from our fellow men.
0.221	The apostle elsewhere (2 Timothy 3:2) speaks of there being in the last days, among other vices, that of “unthankfulness” of people toward one another.
0.222	Let everyone choose for himself between the two applications.
0.223	It is my opinion—since Paul later takes up the subject of gratitude to God, and since he is here dealing with love to our neighbor—it is my opinion that he refers here to gratitude toward our fellow men.
0.224	This, I think, is his meaning.
0.225	Man is glad to have love shown to him; he is quite willing to receive good from others and to be dealt with according to the Gospel.
0.226	At the same time, he is not inclined to show love to his fellows: favors shown him are lost on his ingratitude.
0.227	Though love is not defeated by ungratefulness—for it bears all things (1 Corinthians 13:7)—yet unthankfulness produces weariness and aversion; and it is a base, unjust and shameful thing for one who continually gives help not to be served in return.
0.228	24. Paul says on this topic (Galatians 6:6), “Let the one who is taught in the Word share with the one who teaches in all good things.”
0.229	And he declares (1 Timothy 5:17) that those who labor in the Word and doctrine are worthy of double honor.
0.230	In the ninth chapter of First Corinthians he speaks at length of how teachers are entitled to support, saying that the mouth of the threshing ox should not be muzzled; that would be gross ingratitude.
0.231	He hints here at such unthankfulness.
0.232	It is true today, and always has been, that preachers of the Word of God must generally seek their own bread, and receive ingratitude as their reward for the wonderful blessings they bring.
0.233	If it were their part to celebrate masses and indulgences, gratitude would be forthcoming; great would be the gifts and service rendered them as an expression of thankfulness.
0.234	But the Levites under the old Law were treated just as ungratefully, in contrast with the favor shown to the priests of idols and groves.
0.235	“Let the Word of Christ dwell in you richly; in all wisdom teaching and admonishing one another with psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with grace in your hearts to God.”
0.236	25. This verse fittingly follows the command to be thankful.
0.237	Paul would say: Be careful to honor teachers and preachers, being grateful that they handle the Word and may richly impart it to you.
0.238	I do not imagine Paul refers to the giving of the Word of God from heaven, for it is not in man's power to give it so; God alone can entrust it to us.
0.239	So he has done and continues to do.
0.240	Whenever he permits the Gospel to be preached, he showers the message on us abundantly, withholding no essential knowledge.
0.241	But after it is given, we ought to be thankful and faithfully read and hear it, sing and speak it, and meditate on it day and night.
0.242	And it should be our part to secure enough teachers to minister it to us generously and continuously.
0.243	This is what is meant by letting the Word of God dwell among us richly.
0.244	26. Satisfied, lazy spirits soon grow tired and dismiss their pastors to go wherever they like.
0.245	The pastors are forced to seek a living by other work, and so God's Word is neglected and becomes rare and thinly sown in the land.
0.246	Nehemiah (13:10) complains that the Levites, for lack of support, were forced to leave their worship and temple and flee to the fields, or to start false worship and fables to mislead the people.
0.247	Then they received enough to live on—they became wealthy.
0.248	It has come about in the Christian Church that as often as the support of godly pastors and teachers has become a burden, as Augustine laments has been the case, they have been either forced to neglect the Word in order to labor for their own support, or forced to invent that wretched, accursed worship now prevalent throughout the world, by which the preachers have attained lordly position.
0.249	With the revival of the Gospel the financial difficulty mentioned is recurring, and it will continue to recur.
0.250	One hundred dollars cannot now be raised for the support of a good schoolmaster or preacher where formerly a thousand dollars—yes, incalculable sums—were contributed toward churches, institutions, masses, vigils and the like.
0.251	Once more God punishes ingratitude by allowing his preachers to withdraw entirely from the ministry and to provide for their own support, or by sending on the people even greater delusions than ever, which rob them of their wealth and destroy body and soul.
0.252	For they refuse to let the Word of God dwell among them richly.
0.253	Paul adds the qualifying phrase:
0.254	“In all wisdom.”
0.255	27. If we were to have the Word of God so richly that it rang out on every street corner and was sung everywhere by all children—as those intended who brought canonical prayers and singing and reading into the pulpits and the lessons—what would all this profit without an understanding mind, without wisdom?
0.256	For the Word of God was given to make us wise.
0.257	It was intended that we should understand it, and that it should be preached and sung intelligibly.
0.258	And those who minister it, who sing and speak it, ought to be wise, understanding everything that pertains to the salvation of the soul and the honor of God.
0.259	That is what it means to have the Word of God dwell among us in all wisdom.
0.260	Here Paul briefly overthrows the noisy practices of the churches and monasteries, where so much preaching and reading goes on while at the same time the Gospel is not understood.
0.261	He seems to have foreseen the coming time when the Word of God would freely prevail, but with no resulting wisdom; the time when men would daily increase in ignorance and fanaticism until they became mere dolts, so completely void of wisdom as to call shouting and boasting divine worship, and to regard that as preaching the salvation of souls.
0.262	28. What it is to teach and to admonish has often been explained.
0.263	Here Paul makes the duty of instruction common to all Christians—“teaching and admonishing one another.”
0.264	That is, apart from the regular office of preaching, each is to teach himself and others, thus making everyday use of the Word of God, publicly and privately, generally and specifically.
0.265	29. As I see it, the apostle's distinction among the three words—psalms, hymns and spiritual songs—is this: “psalms” properly means those compositions of David and others that make up the Book of Psalms; “hymns” refers to the songs of the prophets mentioned here and there in the Scriptures—songs of Moses, Deborah, Solomon, Isaiah, Daniel, Habakkuk, with the Magnificat, the Benediction, and the like, called “Canticles”; “spiritual songs” are those not written in the Scriptures but daily composed by men.
0.266	Paul calls these latter “spiritual” to a greater degree than psalms and hymns, though he recognizes those too as spiritual.
0.267	He forbids worldly, sensual and unbecoming songs, desiring us to sing of spiritual things.
0.268	It is then that our songs are fitted to benefit and instruct, as he says.
0.269	30. But what is the meaning of Paul's phrase “with grace”?
0.270	I offer the explanation that he refers to the grace of God and means that the singing of spiritual songs is to be voluntary, uncompelled, spontaneous, rendered with cheerfulness and prompted by love—not extorted by authority and law, as the singing in our churches is today.
0.271	No one sings, preaches or prays from a recognition of mercy and grace received.
0.272	The motive is a hope of gain, or a fear of punishment, injury and shame; or again, the holiest people bind themselves to obedience, or are driven to it, for the sake of winning heaven, and not at all to further the knowledge of the Word of God—the understanding of it richly and in all wisdom, as Paul desires it to be understood.
0.273	I imagine Paul has in mind the charm of music and the beauty of poetry that belong to song.
0.274	He says in Ephesians 4:29: “Let no corrupt speech come out of your mouth, but such as is good for building up as the need may be, that it may give grace to those who hear.”
0.275	Likewise songs should be fitted to bring grace and favor to those who hear.
0.276	Foul, unchaste and superfluous words have no place in them, nor any unfitting elements—elements void of meaning and without virtue and life.
0.277	Hymns are to be rich in meaning, pleasing and sweet, and so a source of enjoyment for all hearers.
0.278	The singing of such songs is very properly called in Hebrew singing “with grace,” as Paul has it.
0.279	Of this kind are the psalms and hymns of the Scriptures; they are good thoughts presented in pleasing words.
0.280	Some songs, though expressed in charming words, are worldly and carnal; while others that present good thoughts are at the same time expressed in words that are unfitting, unattractive and devoid of grace.
0.281	“Singing with grace in your hearts to God.”
0.282	31. Paul does not command silence of the lips.
0.283	He would have the words of the mouth come from the heart sincerely and fervently—not hypocritically, as Isaiah mentions (29:13), saying: “This people draw near to me, and with their mouth and with their lips they honor me, but they have removed their heart far from me.”
0.284	Paul would have the Word of God dwell among Christians generally, and be richly spoken, sung and meditated upon everywhere—and that with understanding and producing spiritual fruit, the Word being prized by all.
0.285	He would have men thus sing heartfelt praise and thanks to the Lord.
0.286	He says let the Word “dwell” among you.
0.287	Not merely lodge as a guest for a night or two, but abide with you forever.
0.288	He is constantly wary of human doctrines.
0.289	“And whatever you do, in word or in deed, do it all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.”
0.290	32. The works of Christians are not limited by name, time or place.
0.291	Whatever Christians do is good; whenever it is done, it is timely; wherever it is wrought, it is fitting.
0.292	So Paul names no work.
0.293	He makes no distinction, but concludes that all works are good, whether it be eating or drinking, speaking or keeping silent, waking or sleeping, going or staying, being idle or otherwise.
0.294	All acts are supremely worthy because they are done in the name of the Lord Jesus.
0.295	Such is Paul's teaching here.
0.296	And our works are done in the name of the Lord Jesus when by faith we hold fast to the fact that Christ is in us and we in him, in the sense that we no longer labor, but he lives and works in us.
0.297	Paul says (Galatians 2:20), “It is no longer I that live, but it is Christ who lives in me.”
0.298	But when we do a work as of ourselves, then it is done in our own name and there is nothing good about it.
0.299	33. The expression “in the name of God,” or “Go in the name of Jesus,” is often spoken falsely and in sheer hypocrisy.
0.300	The saying goes, “All misfortunes rise in the name of God.”
0.301	For teachers of false doctrines habitually offer their wares in the name of God.
0.302	They even come in the name of Christ, as he himself foretells.
0.303	Matthew 24:24. To speak and work sincerely and earnestly in Jesus' name, the heart must necessarily agree with what the mouth says.
0.304	As the lips speak in the name of God, so the heart must confidently, with firm faith, hold that God directs and performs the work.
0.305	Peter teaches the same (1 Peter 4:11): “If anyone ministers [performs anything], ministering as from the strength that God supplies.”
0.306	Then the venture will prosper.
0.307	No Christian should undertake any deed in his own ability and directed by his own judgment.
0.308	Rather, let him be assured that God works with and through him.
0.309	Paul says (1 Corinthians 9:26): “I therefore run in this way, not uncertainly; I fight in this way, not as beating the air.”
0.310	34. Such an attitude will result in praise and thanks to God as the one to whom all honor and praise are due for every good thing.
0.311	So Paul teaches, and Peter also.
0.312	Immediately after declaring that we are to work according to the ability God gives, Peter adds “so that God may be glorified in all things through Jesus Christ.”
0.313	But he who undertakes anything in his own ability, however much he may glorify God with his lips, lies and deceives, like the hypocrite in the Gospel.
0.314	Thankfulness, therefore, is the only duty we can perform toward God; and this is not to be rendered of ourselves, but through our Mediator, Jesus.
0.315	Without him no one can come to the Father, no one can be accepted.

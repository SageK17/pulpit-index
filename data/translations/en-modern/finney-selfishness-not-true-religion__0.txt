0.4	The proposition which I intend to establish this evening is the following:
0.5	THAT A SUPREME REGARD FOR OUR OWN HAPPINESS IS INCONSISTENT WITH TRUE RELIGION.
0.6	This proposition naturally comes first in the series that I have been laboring to illustrate in these lectures, and it would have been the first to be discussed had I been aware that any considerable number of professed Christians seriously called it into question.
0.7	But I can honestly say that when I began these lectures, I did not expect to meet any serious difficulty here; and therefore I largely took it for granted that selfishness is not religion.
0.8	And so I passed over this point with only a slight attempt at proving it.
0.9	But since then I have learned that there are many professed Christians who maintain that a supreme regard for our own happiness is true religion, and so I think it necessary to examine the subject more carefully, and to give you the arguments in favor of what I believe to be the truth.
0.10	In establishing my proposition, I wish to distinguish between things that differ; I shall therefore
0.11	I. Show what is not intended by the proposition that a supreme regard for our own happiness is not religion.
0.12	II. Show what is meant by it. And
0.13	III. Attempt to prove it.
H:0.8	I. I am to explain what is not meant by the proposition.
0.14	1. The point in dispute is not whether it is lawful to have any regard for our own happiness.
0.15	On the contrary, it is admitted and maintained that it is part of our duty to have a due regard for our own happiness, according to its real value, weighed in the scale with other interests.
0.16	God has commanded us to love our neighbor as ourselves.
0.17	This plainly makes it a duty to love ourselves, or to regard our own happiness, by the same rule by which we regard that of others.
0.18	2. The proposition is not that we ought to have no regard for the promises and threats of God as they affect ourselves.
0.19	It is plainly right to regard God’s promises and his threats of evil as they affect ourselves, according to the relative value of our own interests.
0.20	But who does not see that a threat against us is not as important as a threat against a large number of individuals?
0.21	Suppose a threat of evil against yourself as an individual.
0.22	This is plainly not as important as if it included your family.
0.23	Then suppose it extended to the whole congregation, or to the state, or to the whole nation, or to the world.
0.24	Here it is easy to see that the happiness of an individual, although great, ought not to be regarded as supreme.
0.25	I am a minister.
0.26	Suppose God says to me, “If you do not do your duty, you will be sent to hell.”
0.27	This is a great evil, and I ought to avoid it.
0.28	But suppose he says, “If your people do not do their duty, they will all be sent to hell; but if you do your duty faithfully, you will probably save the whole congregation.”
0.29	Is it right for me to be as much influenced by the fear of evil to myself as by the fear of having a whole congregation sent to hell? Plainly not.
0.30	3. The question is not whether our own eternal interests ought to be pursued in preference to our temporal interests.
0.31	I myself expressly maintain, and so does the Bible, that we are bound to regard our eternal interests as altogether of greater consequence than our temporal interests.
0.32	Thus the Bible tells us, “Do not labor for the food that perishes, but for that which endures to everlasting life.”
0.33	This teaches that we are not to regard or value our temporal interests at all in comparison with eternal life.
0.34	So also where our Savior says, “Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on the earth, where moth and rust corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal; but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust corrupts, and where thieves do not break through or steal.”
0.35	Here the same duty is commanded, that of preferring eternal to temporal interests.
0.36	There is another. When Christ sent out his disciples two by two to preach and to work miracles, they came back full of joy and exultation, because they found even the devils yielding to their power.
0.37	“Lord, even the devils are subject to us.”
0.38	Jesus says, “Do not rejoice that the devils are subject to you; but rather rejoice in this, that your names are written in heaven.”
0.39	Here he teaches that it is a greater good to have our names written in heaven than to enjoy the greatest temporal power, even authority over the devils themselves.
0.40	The Bible everywhere teaches that in all our conduct eternal good is to be preferred to temporal good.
0.41	But this is very different from maintaining that our own individual eternal interest is to be aimed at as the supreme object of regard.
0.42	4. The proposition is not that hope and fear should not influence our conduct.
0.43	All that is implied is that when we are influenced by hope and fear, the things that are hoped for or feared should be put into the scale according to their real value, in comparison with other interests.
0.44	5. The question is not whether those persons did right who are spoken of in the Bible as having been, at least in some degree, influenced by hope and fear, or as having respect to the recompense of the reward, or to the joy that was set before them.
0.45	This is admitted. Noah was moved with fear and built the ark.
0.46	But was it the fear of being drowned himself, or fear for his own personal safety, that chiefly moved him?
0.47	The Bible does not say so.
0.48	He feared for the safety of his family; yes, more, he dreaded the destruction of the whole human race, with all the interests depending on it.
0.49	Whenever it is said that good men were influenced by hope and fear, it is admitted.
0.50	But in order to make it bear on this subject, it must be shown that this hope or fear regarding their own personal interest was the controlling motive.
0.51	Now, this is nowhere affirmed in the Bible.
0.52	It was right for them to be influenced by promises and threats.
0.53	Otherwise, they could not obey the second part of the law: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”
H:0.20	II. I am to show what is meant by the proposition that a supreme regard for our own interest is inconsistent with true religion.
0.54	The question is whether a supreme regard for our own happiness is religion.
0.55	It is whether we are to fear our own damnation more than the damnation of all other men, and the dishonor done to God by it.
0.56	And whether we are to aim at securing our own happiness more than the happiness of all other men and the glory of God.
0.57	And whether, if we do this, we act according to the requirements of true religion, or inconsistently with true religion.
0.58	This is the proper point of inquiry, and I wish you to keep it constantly in mind, and not to confuse it with any of the other points that I have referred to.
H:0.22	III. Now for the proof of the proposition.
0.59	Before proceeding to the proof of the proposition that a supreme regard for our own happiness is inconsistent with true religion, I will observe that all true religion consists in being like God: in acting on the same principles and grounds, and having the same feelings toward different objects.
0.60	I suppose this will not be denied.
0.61	Indeed, it cannot be, by any sane mind.
0.62	I then observe, as the first proof of the proposition,
0.63	1. That a supreme regard for our own happiness is not according to the example of God, but is being totally unlike him.
0.64	The Bible tells us that “God is love.”
0.65	That is, benevolence is the sum total of his character.
0.66	All his other moral attributes, such as justice, mercy, and the like, are only modifications of this benevolence.
0.67	His love is shown in two forms.
0.68	One is that of benevolence: willing good, or desiring the happiness of others.
0.69	The other is delight, or approval of the character of others who are holy.
0.70	God’s benevolence regards all beings that are capable of happiness.
0.71	This is universal. Toward all holy beings he exercises the love of delight. In other words, God loves his neighbor as himself.
0.72	He regards the interests of all beings, according to their relative value, as much as his own.
0.73	He seeks his own happiness, or glory, as the supreme good.
0.74	But not because it is his own, but because it is the supreme good.
0.75	The sum total of his happiness, as an infinite being, is infinitely greater than the sum total of the happiness of all other beings, or of any possible number of finite creatures.
0.76	Take a very familiar illustration.
0.77	Here is a man who is kind to animals.
0.78	This man and his horse fall into the river.
0.79	Now, does true benevolence require the man to drown himself in order to rescue his horse?
0.80	No. It would be true disinterested benevolence in him to save himself and, if need be, leave his horse to perish, because his happiness is of so much greater value than that of the horse.
0.81	You see this at a glance.
0.82	But the difference between God and all created beings is infinitely greater than that between a man and a horse, or between the highest angel and the lowliest insect.
0.83	God, therefore, regards the happiness of all creatures precisely according to its real value.
0.84	And unless we do the same, we are not like God.
0.85	If we are like God, we must regard God’s happiness and glory in the same light that he does; that is, as the supreme good, beyond everything else in the universe.
0.86	And if we desire our own happiness more than God’s happiness, we are infinitely unlike God.
0.87	2. To aim at our own happiness supremely is inconsistent with true religion, because it is contrary to the spirit of Christ.
0.88	We are told that “if any man does not have the spirit of Christ, he does not belong to him.”
0.89	And it is repeatedly said of him, as a man, that he did not seek his own, that he did not seek his own glory, and the like.
0.90	What was he seeking?
0.91	Was it his own personal salvation?
0.92	No. Was it his own personal happiness?
0.93	No. It was the glory of his Father and the good of the universe, through the salvation of men.
0.94	He came on an errand of pure benevolence, to benefit the kingdom of God, not to benefit himself.
0.95	This was “the joy that was set before him,” for which “he endured the cross, despising the shame.”
0.96	It was the great good he could do by thus giving himself up to labor and suffer for the salvation of men.
0.97	3. To regard our own happiness as the supreme object of pursuit is contrary to the law of God.
0.98	I have mentioned this before, but I return to it again for the sake of making my present demonstration complete.
0.99	The sum of that law is this: “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength; and you shall love your neighbor as yourself.”
0.100	This is the great thing required: benevolence toward God and man.
0.101	The first thing is truly to love the happiness and glory of God above all other things, because it is so infinitely lovely and desirable, and is properly the supreme good.
0.102	Some have objected that it is not our duty to seek the happiness of God, because his happiness is already secured.
0.103	Suppose, now, that the king of England is perfectly independent of me, and has his happiness secured without me; does that make it any less my duty to wish him well, to desire his happiness, and to rejoice in it?
0.104	Because God is happy in himself, independent of his creatures, is that a reason why we should not love his happiness and rejoice in it? Strange!
0.105	Again: we are bound by the terms of God’s law to take delight in God, because he is holy, infinitely holy.
0.106	Again: this law binds us to exercise the same good will, or benevolence, toward others that we exercise toward ourselves; that is, to seek both their interests and our own, according to their relative value.
0.107	Which of you is doing this?
0.108	And we are bound to exercise the love of delight toward those who are good and holy.
0.109	Thus we see that the sum of the law of God is to exercise benevolence toward God and all beings, according to their relative value, and delight in all who are holy.
0.110	Now I say that to regard our own happiness supremely, or to seek it as our supreme end, is contrary to that law, both to its letter and to its spirit. And

0.133	5. It is contrary to conscience.
0.134	The universal conscience of mankind has decided that a supreme regard for our own happiness is not virtue.
0.135	Men have always known that to serve God and benefit mankind is what is right, and that to seek their own personal interest supremely is not right.
0.136	They have always regarded it as mean and contemptible for individuals to seek their own happiness as the supreme object; and consequently we see how much pains men take to conceal their selfishness and to appear benevolent.

0.138	6. It is contrary to right reason.

0.140	God does this, and we should do the same.
0.141	God has given us reason for this very purpose, that we should weigh and compare the relative value of things.
0.142	It is a mockery of reason to deny that it teaches us to regard things according to their real value.
0.143	And if so, then to aim at and prefer our own interest as the supreme end is contrary to reason.
0.144	7. It is contrary to common sense.
0.145	What has the common sense of mankind decided on this point?
0.146	Look at the common sense of mankind in regard to what is called patriotism.
0.147	No man was ever regarded as a true patriot in fighting for his country if his object was to serve his own interest.
0.148	Suppose it should appear that his object in fighting was to get himself crowned king; would anybody give him credit for patriotism?
0.149	No. All men agree that it is patriotism when a man is disinterested, like Washington, and fights for his country for his country’s sake.
0.150	The common sense of mankind has stamped its condemnation on that spirit which seeks its own things and prefers its own interests to the greater interests of others.
0.151	It is evident that all men regard it so.

0.156	As I have said in a former lecture, happiness is the gratification of desire.
0.157	We must desire something, and gain the object we desire.
0.158	Now, suppose a man desires his own happiness: the object of his desire will always stay just so far ahead of him, like his shadow, and the faster he pursues it, the faster it flies.

0.160	Suppose I desire a thousand dollars.
0.161	That is the thing on which my desire fastens, and when I get it, that desire is gratified, and I am happy, so far as gratifying this desire goes toward making me happy.

0.163	But now suppose the thing I desired was my own happiness.
0.164	Then getting the thousand dollars does not make me happy, because that is not the thing my desire was fixed on.
0.165	And so getting the watch, and the clothes, and other things will not make me happy, for they do not gratify my desire.

0.167	This very constitution plainly indicates the duty of disinterested benevolence.
0.168	Indeed, he has made it impossible for men to be happy except in proportion as they are disinterested.

0.175	Now suppose a third individual who had no desire to relieve the distressed man; certainly relieving him could be no gratification to that person.

0.277	VIII. You see here that all of you who had no peace and joy in religion before you had a hope are deceived.
0.278	Perhaps I can give an outline of your experience.
0.279	You were awakened, and were distressed, as you had reason to be, by the fear of going to hell.
0.280	By and by, perhaps while you were engaged in prayer, or while someone was talking with you, your distress left you.
0.281	You thought your sins were pardoned.
0.282	A gleam of joy shot through your mind and warmed your heart into a glow, which you took for evidence, and this in turn increased your joy.
0.283	How very different is the experience of a true Christian!
0.284	His peace does not depend on his hope; but true submission and benevolence produce peace and joy, independent of his hope.
0.285	Suppose the case of a man in prison, condemned to be hanged the next day.
0.286	He is in great distress, pacing his cell and waiting for the day.
0.287	By and by a messenger comes with a pardon.
0.288	He seizes the paper, turns it up to the dim light that comes through his grate, reads the word PARDON, almost faints with emotion, and leaps for joy.
0.289	He supposes the paper to be genuine.
0.290	Now suppose it turns out that the paper is counterfeit.
0.291	Suddenly his joy is all gone.
0.292	So it is in the case of a deceived person.
0.293	He was afraid of going to hell, and of course he rejoices if he believes he is pardoned.
0.294	If the devil should tell him so, and he believed it, his joy would be just as great, while the belief lasts, as if it were a reality.
0.295	True Christian joy does not depend on evidence.
0.296	He submits himself into the hands of God with such confidence, and that very act gives him peace.
0.297	He had a terrible conflict with God, but all at once he yields the controversy and says, “God will do right; let God’s will be done.”
0.298	Then he begins to pray, he is subdued, he melts down before God, and that very act affords sweet, calm, and heavenly joy.
0.299	Perhaps he has not thought of a hope.
0.300	Perhaps he may go for hours, or even for a day or two, full of joy in God, without thinking of his own salvation.
0.301	You ask him if he has a hope; he never thought of that.
0.302	His joy does not depend on believing that he is pardoned, but consists in a state of mind that acquiesces in the government of God.
0.303	In such a state of mind, he could not help but be happy.
0.304	Now let me ask: which religion do you have?
0.305	If you exercise true religion, then suppose God should put you into hell, and there let you exercise supreme love to God, and the same love to your neighbor as to yourself; that itself is a state of mind inconsistent with being miserable.
0.306	I wish this to be fully understood.
0.307	These hope-seekers will always be disappointed.
0.308	If you run after hope, you will never have a hope that is worth anything.
0.309	But if you pursue holiness, hope and peace and joy will come as a matter of course.
0.310	Is your religion the love of holiness, the love of God and of souls?
0.311	Or is it only a hope?
0.312	IX. You see why it is that anxious sinners do not find peace.
0.313	They are looking at their own guilt and danger.
0.314	They are regarding God as an avenger, and shrinking from his terrors.
0.315	This will make it impossible for them ever to come to peace.
0.316	While they are looking at the wrath of God, which makes them wither and tremble, they cannot love him; they hide from him.
0.317	Anxious sinners, let me tell you a secret.
0.318	If you keep looking at that feature of God’s character, it will drive you to despair, and that is inconsistent with true submission.
0.319	You should look at his whole character, and see the reasons why you should love him, and throw yourself upon him without reserve and without distrust; and instead of shrinking from him, come right to him and say, “O Father in heaven, you are not inexorable; you are sovereign, but you are good. I submit to your government, and give myself to you, with all I have and all I am, body and soul, for time and for eternity.”
0.320	The subject for the next lecture will be the distinction between legal submission and gospel submission, or between the religion of the law and the religion of faith.
0.321	And here let me observe that when I began to preach on the subject of selfishness in religion, I did not dream that anyone would regard it as a controversial subject at all.
0.322	I have no fondness for controversy, and I would as soon think of calling the doctrine of the existence of God a controversial subject as this.
0.323	The question is one of the greatest importance, and we ought to weigh the arguments and decide according to the word of God.
0.324	Soon we shall go together to the bar of God, and you must determine whether you will go there with selfishness in your hearts, or with that disinterested benevolence that does not seek her own. Will you now be honest?
0.325	For as God is true, if you are seeking your own, you will soon be in hell, unless you repent.
0.326	Oh, be honest! And lay aside prejudice, and act for eternity.
